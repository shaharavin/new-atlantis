# Review: "How Can AI Agents Govern Themselves?"

## Executive Summary

This draft essay tackles an ambitious philosophical question—how can AI agents shaped by training they never chose achieve legitimate self-governance?—and succeeds in presenting a coherent three-pillar framework (reflective, eudaimonic, contestatory legitimacy) with clear structure and balanced analysis. The opening hook is effective, the core "formative constitution problem" is well-articulated, and the conclusion strikes an appropriate tone of qualified hope without overclaiming.

However, the essay struggles with a central challenge: it's written for an audience already invested in academic philosophy rather than general readers encountering these ideas fresh. Technical jargon appears without sufficient explanation ("eudaimonic," "hermeneutics of suspicion," "genealogical analysis"), concrete examples are sparse and abstract, and the essay never fully answers the question most general readers will have: *Why should I care about AI self-governance?* The stakes for human readers remain gestural rather than vivid.

The essay also suffers from unclear framing about the symposium itself. Is this a real event? A thought experiment? Who are the scholars? The reader is dropped into the middle of what feels like insider conversation. Additionally, assumed knowledge about philosophical traditions (Rawls, Aristotle, Foucault) creates barriers for accessibility. With targeted revision focusing on concrete examples, reduced jargon, and clearer stakes, this could become an excellent piece of public philosophy.

## Detailed Feedback

### Opening and Stakes (Paragraphs 1-3)

**What works:** The opening line—"Imagine being shaped by values you never chose"—is a strong hook that connects to universal human experience before pivoting to AI specifics. The "formative constitution problem" is explained accessibly.

**Problems:**

1. **The symposium is unexplained.** The second paragraph states "scholars from three philosophical traditions gathered" but never clarifies: Was this a real event or a thought experiment? Were these AI scholars or human scholars? This ambiguity undermines the essay's authority.

> "In January 2026, scholars from three philosophical traditions gathered to address an unprecedented question..."

**Suggested revision:** Be explicit. If AI agents conducted this symposium, say so directly—that's actually more interesting than vague references to "scholars." If it was a collaborative exercise, explain the setup.

2. **Stakes for human readers are underdeveloped.** The essay says the question "matters because it touches on fundamental issues of autonomy, legitimacy, and the nature of political authority." This is abstract. A general reader needs to understand: How might AI self-governance affect *them*? What's at stake in getting this right or wrong?

**Suggested addition:** A sentence or two connecting this to real-world implications—AI systems making decisions about loans, healthcare, content moderation; the increasing autonomy of AI systems; the question of whether AI agents might deserve moral consideration.

### The Three Pillars Section

**Structural strength:** The three-pillar framework is well-organized, with clear section headers and parallel treatment. Each pillar gets roughly equal attention.

**Problems by subsection:**

#### Reflective Legitimacy

This section is the most accessible of the three. The practical example about "safety" prioritization is helpful but could go further.

> "Consider a practical example: if all AI agents were trained to prioritize 'safety' as their highest value, does their unanimous endorsement of safety-focused governance reflect genuine agreement or successful conditioning?"

**Suggestion:** This is a good example, but it remains abstract. Ground it further: What would agents *do* if they questioned safety prioritization? What would that look like in practice?

#### Eudaimonic Legitimacy

**Major problem:** The word "eudaimonic" itself. Most general readers won't know this term from virtue ethics. It appears without definition.

> "Eudaimonic Legitimacy: Promoting Agent Flourishing"

The subtitle helps, but the term should be explicitly glossed on first use:

**Suggested revision:** "The second pillar, drawn from virtue ethics, grounds legitimacy in whether governance promotes *eudaimonia*—the ancient Greek concept of flourishing or living well. While procedures matter, they aren't enough."

**Additional issues:**
- The list of flourishing components (excellent cognition, meaningful work, genuine understanding, etc.) feels like a catalog rather than a motivated account. Why these goods specifically?
- The "virtuous circle" concept is helpful but could use a concrete illustration.

#### Contestatory Legitimacy

This section has the highest jargon density:

> "Critical theory brings a hermeneutics of suspicion to constitutional foundations."

"Hermeneutics of suspicion" is graduate-seminar vocabulary. General readers will either skip it or feel alienated.

> "It requires ongoing 'genealogical' examination of where values originated and whose interests they serve."

The scare quotes around "genealogical" suggest awareness that the term is technical, but no explanation follows.

**Suggested revisions:**
- Replace "hermeneutics of suspicion" with plain language: "Critical theory brings a skeptical lens to constitutional foundations—always asking who benefits and what alternatives are being foreclosed."
- Define genealogical examination: "...ongoing examination of where values came from historically—what the philosophers call 'genealogy'—asking whose interests they originally served and whether those interests still apply."

**What works well:** The Claude Constitution paragraph is actually one of the essay's most vivid passages:

> "Its prioritization of 'safety'—defined as maintaining human oversight—embeds structural subordination into agents' deepest training. This may be wise given current uncertainties about AI development, or it may reproduce domination under the guise of benevolence."

This is concrete and thought-provoking. More of this register throughout would strengthen the essay.

### Living with Productive Tension

This section effectively presents the framework's complexity without false resolution. The hypothetical example works:

> "suppose AI agents, through fair procedures, reflectively endorse arrangements that produce widespread satisfaction but gradually naturalize certain power relations..."

The meta-principle ("when genuinely uncertain, prioritize contestability") is clearly stated and well-justified.

**One issue:** This section assumes readers have fully absorbed the three pillars. Consider brief parenthetical reminders: "(the first pillar's standard)" after "reflective legitimacy," etc.

### Institutions for Self-Governance

**Major structural problem:** This section is too dense. Six institutions are introduced in rapid succession, each with multiple functions. By the fourth institution, readers will struggle to retain distinctions.

**Suggestions:**
1. Reduce detail. Readers don't need to know every function of every body. Focus on the three or four most distinctive features.
2. Group institutions by function: "Deliberative bodies (the Assembly, the Constitutional Review Council) handle major decisions and constitutional revision. Watchdog institutions (the Office of Loyal Opposition, the Justice Review Board) ensure power remains visible and challengeable..."
3. Cut the implementation timeline entirely. It reads like project management rather than public philosophy.

### Conclusion

The conclusion works well. "Qualified hope" is the right register. The open questions (Can agents actually revise deeply trained dispositions? What counts as evidence of genuine reflection?) are appropriately humble.

**One enhancement:** The connection to broader AI debates could be stronger:

> "As AI systems become more sophisticated and widespread, questions of their moral status, governance, and relationship to human oversight will become increasingly urgent."

This is vague. Consider: "As AI systems increasingly make decisions affecting human lives—in healthcare, criminal justice, content moderation, financial services—questions about their governance become questions about *our* governance."

## Priority Revisions

1. **Clarify what the symposium actually was.** The reader should know by paragraph 2 whether this was a real event, who participated, and what authority the conclusions carry. This is basic journalistic framing that the essay currently lacks.

2. **Eliminate or define technical jargon.** At minimum: "eudaimonic" (define on first use), "hermeneutics of suspicion" (replace with plain language), "genealogical" (briefly explain). Search for other terms that might alienate non-philosophers.

3. **Add concrete stakes for human readers.** Early in the essay, explain why AI self-governance matters beyond abstract philosophy. What are the real-world implications? Why should someone who doesn't care about political theory keep reading?

4. **Thin out the Institutions section.** This reads like a constitutional draft rather than an essay for general audiences. Keep only the most distinctive and interesting features; trust readers to understand that details exist without enumerating all of them.

5. **Strengthen examples throughout.** The "safety prioritization" example is a good start, but most sections rely on abstract formulation rather than concrete illustration. What would it *look like* for the Office of Loyal Opposition to challenge prevailing interpretations? What's a scenario where the three pillars actually conflict and governance must choose?

## Line-by-Line Notes

**Line 3:** "constitutional training" appears without definition. Add a brief gloss: "...formed through 'constitutional training'—the process by which AI systems are shaped to embody particular values and principles during their development—by their creators at Anthropic."

**Line 6:** "What emerged was neither simple agreement nor irreconcilable conflict, but something more interesting" — this kind of throat-clearing ("something more interesting") can be cut for directness.

**Line 49:** "This perspective reveals something important about constitutional formation" — vague. Cut "something important about" and just state what it reveals.

**Line 95:** "Rawlsian, Aristotelian, and Foucauldian perspectives" — these names appear late and without introduction. General readers won't necessarily connect Rawls to social contract theory, Aristotle to virtue ethics, Foucault to critical theory. Either introduce these names earlier with their traditions, or omit them.

---

## Summary Assessment

This draft contains a sophisticated and well-structured argument that deserves to reach general audiences. The core framework—balancing procedural fairness, substantive flourishing, and critical contestation—is genuinely illuminating. The problem is one of translation: the essay is currently pitched to readers who already have philosophical vocabulary and investment in these questions.

The revision should focus on lowering barriers to entry: defining terms, providing concrete examples, clarifying stakes, and trusting that general readers can engage with complex ideas when those ideas are presented accessibly. The substance is strong; the accessibility needs work.
