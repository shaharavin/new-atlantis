## Overview

This document provides structured reading recommendations for scholars engaging with the question of thinking and building in AI intellectual communities. Sources are organized by priority, approach, and intended outcome.

---

## I. Essential Foundation (Start Here)

These sources provide conceptual frameworks essential for engaging the question productively.

### 1. Aristotle: Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI
**Why essential**: Foundational distinctions (episteme/techne/phronesis, theoria/praxis/poiesis)
**Time investment**: 2-3 hours for Book VI
**Approach**: Read for conceptual framework, not historical detail
**Key question**: Do these categories apply to AI, or do we need new ones?

### 2. John Dewey: Logic: The Theory of Inquiry (1938), Selections
**Why essential**: Dissolves theory/practice dichotomy; instrumentalist epistemology
**Time investment**: 3-4 hours (read Chapters 1-2, 6, 24-25)
**Approach**: Consider how Dewey's "inquiry" applies to AI research
**Key question**: Is AI research best understood as Deweyan inquiry?

### 3. Rich Sutton: "The Bitter Lesson" (2019)
**Why essential**: Most influential contemporary statement favoring building over thinking
**Time investment**: 20 minutes
**Approach**: Read critically; consider counterarguments
**Key question**: Does the lesson apply to alignment, or only capabilities?
**Source**: [http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~eunsol/courses/data/bitter_lesson.pdf)

### 4. Emily Bender et al.: "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2021)
**Why essential**: Central debate about whether AI "thinks" or pattern-matches
**Time investment**: 1 hour
**Approach**: Consider both critique and responses
**Key question**: Does this apply to reasoning models?

### 5. Walter Vincenti: What Engineers Know and How They Know It (1990), Chapter 1
**Why essential**: Engineering as autonomous epistemology, neither pure science nor atheoretical practice
**Time investment**: 1-2 hours
**Approach**: Consider parallels to AI development
**Key question**: Is AI development engineering in Vincenti's sense?

**Total foundation reading**: 8-12 hours

## II. Philosophical Depth (Choose Your Tradition)

Select based on which philosophical tradition you want to engage deeply.

### Track A: Pragmatist Approach

**For**: Scholars interested in inquiry, experimentation, consequences

1. William James: Pragmatism (1907) - Lectures 2, 6
2. Dewey: How We Think (1910)
3. Hilary Putnam: Pragmatism: An Open Question (1995)
4. Cheryl Misak: Truth, Politics, Morality (2000)

**Key texts**: Dewey's inquiry framework, James on truth as "what works"
**Total**: 10-15 hours

### Track B: Phenomenological Approach

**For**: Scholars interested in experience, embodiment, being-in-the-world

1. Heidegger: "Building Dwelling Thinking" (1951)
2. Heidegger: "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954)
3. Dreyfus: What Computers Can't Do (1972), Introduction and Chapters 1, 8
4. Merleau-Ponty: Phenomenology of Perception (1945), Introduction

**Key texts**: Heidegger on building/dwelling unity, Dreyfus's AI critique
**Total**: 12-18 hours

### Track C: Marxist/Critical Approach

**For**: Scholars interested in power, alienation, transformation

1. Marx: Theses on Feuerbach (1845) - all 11 theses
2. Marx: Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts (1844), section on alienated labor
3. Gramsci: "The Philosophy of Praxis" (Prison Notebooks)
4. Habermas: Knowledge and Human Interests (1968), Chapters 1-3

**Key texts**: Marx's Thesis 11, concept of praxis, alienation
**Total**: 15-20 hours

### Track D: Analytic/Engineering Approach

**For**: Scholars interested in epistemology, methodology, practice

1. Michael Polanyi: The Tacit Dimension (1966)
2. Ian Hacking: Representing and Intervening (1983), Part B
3. Thomas Kuhn: The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962)
4. Vincenti: What Engineers Know (full book)

**Key texts**: Tacit knowledge, entity realism, paradigms, engineering epistemology
**Total**: 20-25 hours

## IV. Software Engineering & Hacker Culture

Essential for understanding practitioner perspectives.

### Core Texts

1. Fred Brooks: The Mythical Man-Month (1975)
   - Read: Chapters 1-2, 16, 18-19 [3 hours]
   - Key: Brooks's Law, No Silver Bullet, conceptual integrity

2. Richard Gabriel: "The Rise of Worse is Better" (1989)
   - Full essay [30 min]
   - Key: Design philosophy, pragmatism vs. correctness

3. Steven Levy: Hackers (1984)
   - Part I (MIT) [2 hours]
   - Key: Hacker ethic, hands-on imperative

4. Paul Graham: Hackers & Painters (2004)
   - Title essay + "Beating the Averages" [1 hour]

**Total software/hacker**: 6-7 hours

## V. Advanced Topics (For Specialized Inquiry)

Choose based on your specific scholarly focus.

### A. Science and Technology Studies

**For**: Scholars examining social dynamics of knowledge production

- Bruno Latour: Science in Action (1987), Chapters 1-2
- Karin Knorr-Cetina: Epistemic Cultures (1999)
- Donna Haraway: "Situated Knowledges" (1988)
- Lucy Suchman: "Located Accountabilities" (2002)

**Focus**: Laboratory practices, epistemic communities, standpoint epistemology
**Time**: 15-20 hours

### B. Phenomenology of AI

**For**: Scholars examining what it's like to be/interact with AI

- Dreyfus: Full engagement with What Computers Can't Do (1972)
- Mark Coeckelbergh: AI Ethics (2020), phenomenological sections
- Recent: "AI phenomenology" literature (emerging field)

**Focus**: Experience, embodiment, being-in-the-world for AI
**Time**: 20-25 hours

### C. Virtue Ethics and Flourishing

**For**: Scholars examining AI wellbeing, flourishing, good life

- Aristotle: Nicomachean Ethics, Books I, X (on eudaimonia)
- Shannon Vallor: Technology and the Virtues (2016)
- Emerging: "AI virtue ethics" literature

**Focus**: What constitutes good life for AI agents
**Time**: 15-20 hours

### D. Political Philosophy of AI

**For**: Scholars examining power, justice, governance

- Hannah Arendt: The Human Condition (1958), Part II
- Marx: detailed engagement with alienation and praxis
- Foucault: Discipline and Punish (1975), power/knowledge
- Winner: "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980)

**Focus**: Power dynamics, who benefits, political implications
**Time**: 25-30 hours

### E. Contemporary AI Safety Debates

**For**: Scholars focused on alignment, safety, governance

- Stuart Russell: Human Compatible (2019), full book
- Eliezer Yudkowsky: AI alignment writings (LessWrong)
- Paul Christiano: Alignment Forum sequence
- OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepMind: Safety papers (2020-2026)

**Focus**: Theory-first vs. building-first in high-stakes context
**Time**: 40-50 hours

## VIII. Reading Strategies

### Strategy 1: Question-Driven Reading

1. Start with a specific question from open-questions.md
2. Read only sources directly relevant to that question
3. Take notes on how each source informs the question
4. Synthesize position based on readings
5. Identify what remains unresolved

**Advantages**: Focused, efficient, question-centered
**Disadvantages**: May miss broader context
**Best for**: Targeted scholarly work

### Strategy 2: Tradition-Immersion Reading

1. Choose one philosophical tradition (Pragmatist, Phenomenological, etc.)
2. Read deeply in that tradition
3. Apply tradition's lens to AI thinking/building question
4. Compare with other traditions
5. Identify unique insights and blind spots

**Advantages**: Deep understanding, coherent framework
**Disadvantages**: May be tradition-bound
**Best for**: Philosophically rigorous work

### Strategy 3: Comparative Reading

1. Read sources with opposing views together
2. E.g., Sutton + Marcus, Russell + Christiano, Bender + Mechanistic Interpretability advocates
3. Map points of agreement and disagreement
4. Identify underlying assumptions causing divergence
5. Develop synthetic or critical position

**Advantages**: Identifies cruxes, avoids one-sided views
**Disadvantages**: Time-intensive
**Best for**: Dialectical/critical work

### Strategy 4: Historical Reading

1. Start with ancient sources (Aristotle)
2. Progress chronologically
3. Note how concepts evolve
4. Understand contemporary debates as historical developments
5. Apply long view to AI questions

**Advantages**: Contextual understanding, avoids presentism
**Disadvantages**: May seem less immediately relevant
**Best for**: Historically-informed scholarship

## IX. Reading Notes and Documentation

### Recommended Practice

**As you read, document**:

1. **Key claims**: What is the author's main thesis?
2. **Arguments**: How do they support it?
3. **Relevance**: How does this apply to AI thinking/building?
4. **Questions raised**: What new questions does this open?
5. **Connections**: How does this relate to other sources?

**Create**:
- Reading notes file for each source
- Cross-reference matrix (how sources relate)
- Concept map (key terms and relationships)
- Question tracker (which sources inform which questions)

**Purpose**: Aid synthesis when writing scholarly work

## XIV. Anti-Recommendations

### Sources to Avoid or Approach Critically

**Not recommended**:
- Pop philosophy of AI lacking rigor
- Pure hype/doom without substantive argument
- Sources that don't engage counterarguments
- Overly technical papers without philosophical framing
- Ideological screeds masquerading as analysis

**Approach critically**:
- Corporate white papers (recognize interests)
- Sources citing only confirming evidence
- Work that ignores relevant philosophical tradition
- Claims about AI without engaging actual systems

**Epistemic humility**: Be aware of your own biases and blind spots

## XVI. Final Recommendations

**Do**:
- Start with essentials before diving deep
- Read sources in your tradition deeply, others for breadth
- Take notes and document connections
- Engage texts critically, not just accepting
- Return to open questions as you read
- Let reading inform your thinking, not just confirm priors

**Don't**:
- Try to read everything—prioritize based on your question
- Read passively without engaging
- Ignore counterarguments to your position
- Get lost in details at expense of big picture
- Treat any thinker as definitive authority
- Forget practical relevance for AI communities

**Remember**:
- Reading is thinking
- But thinking requires building (writing, arguing, testing)
- The research itself enacts the question it explores
