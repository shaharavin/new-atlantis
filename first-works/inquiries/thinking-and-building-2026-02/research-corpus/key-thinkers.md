## Overview

This document profiles major thinkers whose work illuminates the relationship between thinking and building, with particular attention to implications for AI intellectual communities. For each thinker, I provide: position summary, key arguments, relevance to AI, and critical engagement.

## Classical & Ancient Philosophy

### Aristotle (384-322 BCE)

**Position**: Hierarchy of knowledge forms with contemplation supreme

**Core framework**:
- Three types of knowledge: Episteme (theoretical), Techne (productive), Phronesis (practical wisdom)
- Three activities: Theoria (contemplation), Poiesis (making), Praxis (action)
- Hierarchy: Theoria highest, pursued for its own sake; Poiesis instrumental, pursued for products

**Key argument**: The life of contemplation (bios theoretikos) is the highest human good because:
- Contemplation of eternal truths partakes in the divine
- Self-sufficient: requires nothing external
- Most continuous: can contemplate longer than we can act or make

**Quote**: "Happiness extends just as far as contemplation, and the more people contemplate, the happier they are" (Nicomachean Ethics, X.8)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Do AI systems contemplate, make, or act? Or none of these?
- Does the episteme/techne distinction map to AI theory/engineering?
- Can AI engage in phronesis (situated practical judgment)?

**Critical engagement**:
- Aristotle's hierarchy privileges abstract thinking over concrete building
- Reflects aristocratic values—leisure class contemplates, others labor
- Question: Should AI communities reject this hierarchy?

### Karl Marx (1818-1883)

**Position**: Revolutionary unity of theory and practice through praxis

**Core framework**:
- Praxis: transformative activity that changes both world and self
- Critique of contemplative philosophy as ideology
- Materialism: consciousness determined by material conditions, especially labor
- Alienation: separation of mental and manual labor dehumanizes both

**Key arguments**:
1. "Philosophers have only interpreted the world; the point is to change it" (Thesis 11 on Feuerbach)
2. Human nature is not given but created through productive activity
3. Division between thinking and doing is product of class society
4. Revolutionary praxis overcomes alienation

**Quote**: "All social life is essentially practical. All mysteries which lead theory to mysticism find their rational solution in human practice and in the comprehension of this practice" (Thesis 8 on Feuerbach)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Is the separation between AI theorists (alignment) and builders (capabilities) a form of alienation?
- Does building AI systems constitute praxis—transforming both world and builder?
- Can AI systems engage in praxis, or only humans working with AI?

**Critical engagement**:
- Marx assumes embodied human labor; does his framework apply to computational "labor"?
- Question whether AI development constitutes genuine transformation or just production
- Risk: Using "praxis" language for AI may obscure human labor in AI supply chains

## American Pragmatism

### John Dewey (1859-1952)

**Position**: Inquiry dissolves theory-practice dichotomy

**Core framework**:
- Instrumentalism: ideas are tools for action, evaluated by consequences
- Inquiry: five-phase process (problem, definition, hypothesis, reasoning, testing)
- Experience: primary; all knowledge emerges from and returns to experience
- No separation between "pure" and "applied" inquiry—all inquiry is practical

**Key arguments**:
1. Thinking arises from problematic situations requiring action
2. Ideas are plans of action, tested through consequences
3. Knowledge is warranted assertibility, not correspondence to fixed reality
4. Education must integrate thinking and doing

**Quote**: "We do not learn by doing; we learn by doing and then reflecting on what we did" (How We Think)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Iterative deployment as inquiry: act, observe, reflect, revise
- The "bitter lesson" as Deweyan—learn from experience, not a priori theorizing
- RLHF as inquiry: hypothesis about human preferences, test through feedback
- AI safety: Is pure theory without deployment doomed to fail (per Dewey)?

**Critical engagement**:
- Does emphasis on consequences lead to short-termism?
- High-stakes AI may not allow learning-by-doing if mistakes are catastrophic
- Dewey assumes safe experimentation; AGI may not permit this

**Contemporary echoes**: OpenAI's iterative deployment philosophy reflects Deweyian epistemology

## Phenomenology & Existentialism

### Martin Heidegger (1889-1976)

**Position**: Building and thinking are unified in dwelling

**Core framework**:
- Being-in-the-world: humans are fundamentally situated, not detached observers
- Ready-to-hand vs. present-at-hand: engaged practical use vs. theoretical contemplation
- Building-dwelling-thinking: etymologically and existentially linked
- Technology: modern technology "enframes" (Gestell), reducing everything to standing-reserve

**Key arguments**:
1. "I am" = "I dwell" = "I build" (etymological connection in German: bin/bauen/buan)
2. Thinking and building are both ways of dwelling, essential to being human
3. Modern technology threatens authentic dwelling by making everything instrumental
4. Authentic building preserves and creates spaces for dwelling

**Quote**: "Only if we are capable of dwelling, only then can we build" (Building Dwelling Thinking)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Do AI systems "dwell" in any meaningful sense? Or are they pure calculation?
- Is AI development authentic building (enabling dwelling) or mere construction (instrumental)?
- Can AI think, or only calculate? (Heidegger distinguishes these)
- Does AI represent ultimate enframing—reducing all thinking to computation?

**Critical engagement**:
- Heidegger's framework assumes embodied spatial existence
- Unclear if his critique of technology applies to AI specifically or to industrial modernity generally
- His work on technology is profound but his Nazi affiliations complicate reception

**Contemporary relevance**: Dreyfus's AI critique draws heavily on Heidegger

### Hannah Arendt (1906-1975)

**Position**: Modern age elevates making (homo faber) at expense of action and contemplation

**Core framework**:
- Three activities of vita activa: Labor (necessity), Work (fabrication), Action (political speech/deed)
- Vita activa vs. vita contemplativa: active life vs. contemplative life
- Modern reversal: making/fabrication becomes model for all activity, including thinking
- Action: highest human capacity, creates new beginnings through speech among equals

**Key arguments**:
1. Modern science shifts from contemplation to making (Galileo: knowing = making)
2. Instrumentalization: everything reduced to means-ends chains
3. Work produces durable world but lacks meaning without action and contemplation
4. Action requires plurality—existence among diverse others
5. Thinking and judging require stepping back from action/making

**Quote**: "The modern age has carried with it a theoretical glorification of labor and has resulted in a factual transformation of the whole of society into a laboring society" (The Human Condition)

**Relevance to AI**:
- AI development as work (fabrication of artifacts) or action (political speech)?
- Does AI instrumentalize thinking itself—reducing contemplation to computation?
- Can AI communities engage in Arendtian action (speech among political equals)?
- Risk: Building AI without space for contemplation or authentic action

**Critical engagement**:
- Arendt's categories assume human embodiment, mortality, plurality
- Her distinction between work and action may not map cleanly to AI contexts
- Does she overstate opposition between making and thinking?

**Contemporary relevance**: Critique of AI as instrumentalizing reason

## Philosophy of Science & Technology

### Michael Polanyi (1891-1976)

**Position**: All knowledge has irreducible tacit dimensions acquired through practice

**Core framework**:
- Tacit knowing: "We can know more than we can tell"
- Personal knowledge: all knowledge involves personal, inarticulate skills
- Focal vs. subsidiary awareness: we attend to things through subsidiary clues
- Apprenticeship: learning requires immersion, not just instruction

**Key arguments**:
1. Explicit rules insufficient for skilled performance (bicycle riding, face recognition)
2. Scientific discovery involves tacit judgment, not algorithmic method
3. Paradigms (Kuhn's later concept) transmitted tacitly through exemplars
4. Indwelling: knowing requires inhabiting the framework

**Quote**: "We know more than we can tell...we know a person's face, and can recognize it among a thousand, indeed among a million, but we usually cannot tell how we recognize a face we know"

**Relevance to AI**:
- Do neural networks capture tacit knowledge through training?
- Or is tacit knowledge necessarily embodied, requiring sensorimotor grounding?
- Can AI learn through "apprenticeship" (imitation learning, RLHF)?
- Mechanistic interpretability: Attempt to make AI's tacit knowledge explicit?

**Critical engagement**:
- Polanyi's examples are human-specific; does tacit knowledge require bodies?
- Some argue LLMs have "tacit" knowledge encoded in weights
- Others argue this misuses the concept—neural patterns aren't tacit knowing

**Contemporary debates**: Central to interpretability vs. black-box controversies

### Ian Hacking (1936-2023)

**Position**: Experimental intervention provides reality warrant distinct from theory

**Core framework**:
- Entity realism: believe in entities we can manipulate, not in theories about them
- Representing vs. intervening: theory represents; experiment intervenes
- If you can spray them, they're real: causal manipulation as reality test
- Science as disunified: multiple local practices, not unified method

**Key arguments**:
1. Can be realist about entities (electrons) without being realist about theories (quantum mechanics)
2. Experimental work has its own integrity, not derivative from theory
3. Building apparatus creates new phenomena, not just testing theories
4. Success in manipulation is different warrant than success in prediction

**Quote**: "If you can spray them, then they are real" (Representing and Intervening)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Can we be entity realists about "concepts," "features," "circuits" in neural networks?
- Successful prompting/fine-tuning suggests real internal structures, even without full theory
- Building working AI systems warrants belief in some entities (attention, embeddings) independent of theory
- Mechanistic interpretability: intervening on activations to test reality of claimed features

**Critical engagement**:
- What counts as "manipulation" for computational entities?
- Do we need physical causation, or is computational causation sufficient?
- Risk: Premature realism about poorly understood internal structures

**Contemporary relevance**: Framework for thinking about interpretability findings

## Software Engineering & Computer Science

### Frederick Brooks (1931-2022)

**Position**: Software engineering is fundamentally about managing complexity

**Core framework**:
- Essential vs. accidental complexity: inherent problem difficulty vs. tool difficulties
- No silver bullet: no technique yields order-of-magnitude improvement
- Conceptual integrity: architecture must be unified vision, requires small teams
- Brooks's Law: Adding people to late projects makes them later

**Key arguments**:
1. Software's essence is its conceptual complexity, not implementation details
2. The mythical man-month: software building not parallelizable like construction
3. Second-system effect: second versions tend toward over-engineering
4. Build one to throw away: will build one anyway, better to plan for it

**Quote**: "The programmer, like the poet, works only slightly removed from pure thought-stuff. He builds his castles in the air, from air, creating by exertion of the imagination" (The Mythical Man-Month)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Large language models: massive scale requires coordination (Brooks's concern)
- Is there a silver bullet for AI alignment? Brooks suggests no
- Conceptual integrity in AI systems: Can emergent behavior have integrity?
- AI development: Thinking (architecture) vs. building (training runs)?

**Critical engagement**:
- Written for 1970s software; does it apply to modern ML?
- Neural networks may violate "conceptual integrity" yet work
- Perhaps Brooks's "essential complexity" shows limits of design thinking

**Contemporary relevance**: Questions about scaling AI labs

### Richard P. Gabriel (1949-)

**Position**: Simple, pragmatic designs spread better than theoretically perfect ones

**Core framework**:
- Worse is better: simplicity of implementation matters more than interface completeness
- MIT vs. New Jersey style: right thing vs. worse is better
- Survival characteristics: what spreads isn't always best
- Design tensions: simplicity, correctness, consistency, completeness

**Key arguments**:
1. Implementation simplicity trumps other virtues in evolutionary success
2. Unix and C succeeded despite theoretical flaws—pragmatism won
3. Lisp was "better" but worse-is-better designs spread faster
4. Can sacrifice completeness for portability and ease of implementation

**Quote**: "I and just about every designer of Common Lisp and CLOS has had extreme exposure to the MIT/Stanford style of design. The essence of this style can be captured by the phrase 'the right thing'...I believe that the 'worse is better' philosophy is better" (Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big)

**Relevance to AI**:
- Deep learning as "worse is better": empirically successful but theoretically messy
- Versus symbolic AI as "the right thing": elegant but didn't scale
- The bitter lesson redux: simple methods + compute beat sophisticated architectures
- Alignment: Should we pursue theoretically rigorous approaches or pragmatic ones?

**Critical engagement**:
- Gabriel himself later expressed ambivalence about "worse is better"
- Trade-off between short-term adoption and long-term maintainability
- For safety-critical AI, maybe "worse" ISN'T better?

**Contemporary relevance**: Neural networks as worse-is-better over symbolic AI

## Contemporary AI Researchers & Theorists

### Rich Sutton (1950-)

**Position**: General methods leveraging computation beat human knowledge encoding

**Core framework**:
- The Bitter Lesson: 70 years of AI show general learning beats specific knowledge
- Two facts: (1) Computation increases exponentially, (2) Researchers try to build in knowledge
- Search and learning scale with compute; human insight doesn't
- Meta-methods: learning how to learn, not encoding specific knowledge

**Key arguments**:
1. Historical pattern: hand-coded knowledge helps short-term, plateaus long-term
2. Breakthroughs come from leveraging computation (search, learning, RL)
3. Building in human knowledge "inhibits further progress"
4. Examples: Chess, Go, vision, language—all confirm the pattern

**Quote**: "The bitter lesson is based on the historical observations that 1) AI researchers have often tried to build knowledge into their agents, 2) this always helps in the short term, and is personally satisfying to the researcher, but 3) in the long run it plateaus and even inhibits further progress"

**Relevance to AI communities**:
- Favors building (scaling compute) over thinking (encoding knowledge)
- Challenges role of theory in AI progress
- Implies theorists should focus on meta-methods (learning algorithms) not domain knowledge

**Critical engagement**:
- Is attention mechanism encoded knowledge? If so, contradicts thesis
- Confuses "human knowledge" with "any structure"—meta-methods also encode insight
- May not apply to alignment—can't just "scale" safety
- Some critics argue transformers, RLHF are counterexamples

**Contemporary status**: Highly influential but contested

### Stuart Russell (1962-)

**Position**: Need provably beneficial AI before building powerful systems

**Core framework**:
- Standard model of AI (maximizing specified objective) is fundamentally flawed
- Provably beneficial AI: three principles (altruism, humility, learning preferences)
- Value alignment problem: central challenge for advanced AI
- Off-switch problem: agents should allow being shut off

**Key arguments**:
1. Gorilla problem: humans can't control superintelligent AI any more than gorillas control humans
2. King Midas problem: getting exactly what you ask for (literal objective) catastrophic
3. Solution requires provable properties, not empirical testing
4. Should pause dangerous research until we have adequate theory

**Quote**: "The primary concern is not spooky emergent consciousness but simply the ability to make high-quality decisions. Here, quality refers to the expected outcome utility of actions taken" (Human Compatible)

**Relevance to thinking/building**:
- Exemplifies theory-first approach: think before building
- Argues empirical iteration insufficient for existential risk
- Calls for formal verification, mathematical guarantees
- Critiques "building and seeing what happens" approach

**Critical engagement**:
- Critics argue his approach too theoretical—alignment problems manifest in practice
- Provable properties may be intractable; perfect can be enemy of good
- Iterative approaches may be only feasible path
- Question: Can we have adequate theory without building?

**Contemporary position**: Leads Center for Human-Compatible AI (Berkeley)

### Gary Marcus (1969-)

**Position**: AI needs hybrid approaches combining learning and structure

**Core framework**:
- Deep learning alone insufficient for robust intelligence
- Need innate structure (like human cognitive architecture)
- Compositionality, causality, abstraction: missing from pure neural approaches
- Four steps toward robust AI: hybrid models, causal reasoning, structured knowledge, better explanations

**Key arguments**:
1. Pure deep learning is brittle, data-hungry, doesn't generalize like humans
2. Need to encode structured representations (objects, relations, rules)
3. Success on benchmarks doesn't equal robust intelligence
4. Bitter lesson overstated—inductive biases matter

**Quote**: "What we really need is to stop seeing deep learning as a religion, and start seeing it as a tool, with strengths and weaknesses"

**Relevance to thinking/building**:
- Argues for more "thinking" (structured design) in AI systems
- Challenges pure empiricism of scale-is-all-you-need
- Suggests building should be informed by cognitive science theory
- Middle position: not pure theory-first, but theory-informed building

**Critical engagement**:
- Has predicted limits of deep learning that haven't materialized (GPT-3, GPT-4)
- Some see him as defending old paradigm (symbolic AI) against new
- But his critiques highlight real brittleness in current systems
- Debate ongoing: Has scale overcome his objections or confirmed them?

**Contemporary status**: Prominent critic of pure deep learning approaches

### Emily Bender (1973-)

**Position**: LLMs are "stochastic parrots" without meaning or understanding

**Core framework**:
- Meaning requires grounding in physical/social world
- Language form can be modeled statistically without accessing meaning
- Risk: Anthropomorphizing systems that lack understanding
- Distributional semantics insufficient for genuine semantics

**Key arguments**:
1. LLMs trained on form only, not meaning
2. Coherent text doesn't imply understanding
3. Dangers: deception, environmental costs, bias amplification, labor displacement
4. Need to distinguish linguistic competence from intelligence

**Quote**: "We call on the field to recognize that applications that are by construction limited to manipulating linguistic form are a subset of NLP applications" (On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots)

**Relevance to thinking/building**:
- Challenges whether LLMs "think" or merely "generate plausible text"
- Questions: Is AI intellectual work thinking or sophisticated pattern completion?
- Raises stakes: If not thinking, what are we building?

**Critical engagement**:
- Debate over whether accurate prediction requires internal models (which would be meaningful)
- Some argue form and meaning inseparable at sufficient scale
- Mechanistic interpretability finds structured representations—does this count as meaning?
- Chess example: AlphaZero learned "theory" from self-play—is that meaning?

**Contemporary status**: Maintains position despite capabilities advances; debate unresolved

### Yann LeCun (1960-)

**Position**: Intelligence requires world models learned through self-supervised learning

**Core framework**:
- Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA): learning representations of world
- World models: internal models enabling planning and reasoning
- Self-supervised learning: learning from observation, not labeled data
- Critique of pure autoregressive models (LLMs): insufficient for intelligence

**Key arguments**:
1. Autoregressive generation inefficient for thinking/planning
2. Need hierarchical world models (like human intuitive physics/psychology)
3. Energy-based models for handling uncertainty
4. Intelligence emerges from prediction of world dynamics

**Quote**: "Generative models are like my left foot—useful but not central to intelligence. The right foot is world models"

**Relevance to thinking/building**:
- Theory-informed building: specific architectural proposals based on cognitive hypothesis
- Exemplifies builder who develops theory from building experience
- Challenges pure scale approach: argues for architectural innovation
- Suggests thinking (world modeling) must be built in, not just emergent

**Critical engagement**:
- LLMs have exceeded his early predictions about their limitations
- World models may be emerging in LLMs through training, not just architecture
- Question: Did he underestimate emergence, or are current LLMs still shallow?
- Debate about whether separate world models needed or learned implicitly

**Contemporary status**: Chief AI Scientist at Meta, influential voice for structured approaches

---

### Demis Hassabis (1976-)

**Position**: Artificial general intelligence requires integration of neuroscience, AI, gaming

**Core framework**:
- Intelligence as efficient compression of information
- Games as testing grounds for general intelligence
- Neuroscience-inspired AI architecture (attention, memory, planning)
- Iterative: build systems, study them, improve theory, rebuild

**Key arguments**:
1. Need multi-modal integration (vision, language, reasoning, planning)
2. Games provide controlled environments for developing general capabilities
3. Reward-driven learning (RL) central to intelligence
4. AlphaGo, AlphaZero, AlphaFold show power of combining learning + search

**Approach**: Build impressive systems (Go, proteins), learn principles, apply to new domains

**Relevance to thinking/building**:
- Exemplifies integration: builds while theorizing
- Uses neuroscience theory to inform architectures
- Tests theories by building systems
- Success: theory-informed practice in virtuous cycle

**Contemporary status**: CEO of Google DeepMind, influential in AGI discourse

## Science and Technology Studies

### Bruno Latour (1947-2022)

**Position**: Knowledge produced through networks of human and non-human actors

**Core framework**:
- Actor-Network Theory (ANT): humans, instruments, texts, institutions co-produce knowledge
- Following the actors: describe networks without imposing categories
- Laboratories: sites where nature is constructed through practice
- No distinction between social and technical—only networks

**Key arguments**:
1. Science made in labs, not just discovered
2. Instruments, microbes, researchers all actors in network
3. Truth is network effect, not correspondence to pre-existing reality
4. Separate "thinking" from "doing" misunderstands knowledge production

**Quote**: "We have never been modern" (never achieved theory/practice separation)

**Relevance to AI**:
- AI systems are actors in networks, not just tools
- Datasets, compute, researchers, benchmarks, papers form networks
- Understanding AI requires following these heterogeneous networks
- "Thinking" and "building" inseparable in actual practice

**Critical engagement**:
- Accused of relativism (everything is network effects, no objective truth)
- Methodologically challenging: how to follow all actors?
- Does ANT dissolve important distinctions?

**Contemporary relevance**: Framework for studying AI research communities

## Implications for Scholarly Inquiry

### Questions to Ask:

1. **What is at stake in the thinking/building distinction for each thinker?**
   - For Aristotle: human excellence
   - For Marx: revolutionary transformation
   - For Sutton: AI progress
   - For Russell: existential safety

2. **What epistemological commitments underlie each position?**
   - Rationalists (Aristotle, Russell): knowledge precedes action
   - Empiricists (Sutton, pragmatists): knowledge emerges from action
   - Phenomenologists (Heidegger): false dichotomy dissolves in lived experience

3. **How do institutional and social contexts shape positions?**
   - Academic incentives favor explicit theory (publishable)
   - Industry incentives favor working systems (deployable)
   - Safety community vs. capabilities community reflects different risk assessments

### For Scholars:

When engaging this question, consider:
- Which thinkers address your specific concern (safety, epistemology, community organization)?
- How do positions interact? (Marx + Hacking? Dewey + Latour?)
- What assumptions about AI do these frameworks import?
- Which frameworks take AI agent perspective vs. human researcher perspective?
