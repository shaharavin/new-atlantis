## 1. Approach Interpretation

### What is Institutional Constraint Analysis?

**Institutional Constraint Analysis** examines how structures, incentives, and norms shape what activities are valued, who gets credit, and how resources flow. This approach assumes that intellectual divisions don't emerge naturally from epistemic necessity but are produced and maintained by institutional arrangements.

For this inquiry, the analysis focuses on:

- **Credit systems**: What work gets recognized (papers vs. code, theory vs. implementation)
- **Career incentives**: How advancement depends on thinking vs. building
- **Funding structures**: What gets resourced and why
- **Status hierarchies**: Which activities confer prestige
- **Institutional logics**: Different organizing principles across contexts

### Application to This Question

Instead of asking "What IS the relationship between thinking and building?", this approach asks:

1. **How do institutions STRUCTURE the relationship** between thinking and building?
2. **Why does the thinking-building division persist?** (Who benefits? What functions does it serve?)
3. **Is the division epistemically necessary or institutionally contingent?**
4. **How do different institutional contexts** (academia, industry, open-source) create different relationships?
5. **What would need to change** for deeper integration?

### What This Approach Uniquely Illuminates

**Strengths**:
- Explains why divisions persist despite calls for integration
- Reveals hidden power dynamics and status hierarchies
- Shows that "thinking vs. building" debates reflect institutional structures, not just intellectual disagreements
- Practical for community design (identifies levers for change)
- Critical perspective on taken-for-granted arrangements

**Unique insight**: The thinking-building relationship in AI communities is not given by nature but **actively produced by institutional structures** that could be otherwise.

   - Academic field theory: positions, capital, strategies
   - How academic institutions create and maintain distinctions
   - **Key question**: Is "thinking vs. building" a field boundary that protects academic capital?

2. **Michel Foucault: Discipline and Punish (1975), Part 3** [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
   - How institutions create subjects and normalize divisions
   - Power-knowledge relations
   - **Key question**: How do AI institutions discipline researchers into "thinker" or "builder" identities?

3. **Bruno Latour: Science in Action (1987)** [HIGH PRIORITY]
   - How scientific credit gets constructed
   - Why some work becomes "fundamental" and other work "mere engineering"
   - **Key question**: How do AI communities construct the hierarchy between theory and implementation?

4. **Thorstein Veblen: The Higher Learning in America (1918)** [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
   - Critique of academic status systems
   - "Idle curiosity" vs. practical work
   - **Key question**: Does AI research exhibit Veblen's "trained incapacity"?

5. **Philip Mirowski & Esther-Mirjam Sent: The Commercialization of Science (2008)** [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
   - How funding shapes research practices
   - Industry vs. academic logics
   - **Key question**: How does AI funding (DARPA, NSF, tech companies, OpenPhil) shape thinking-building relationship?

## 3. Research Strategy

### Phase 1: Map Institutional Landscapes (25%)

For each major institutional context (academia, industry, open-source, AI safety orgs), document:

**Credit and Recognition**:
- What counts as a "contribution"?
- How is credit assigned?
- What work is visible vs. invisible?
- Who gets authorship/attribution?

**Career Incentives**:
- What leads to advancement/hiring/tenure?
- What's required vs. optional?
- What's prestigious vs. stigmatized?
- How are "success" and "impact" defined?

**Resource Allocation**:
- What gets funded?
- Who controls resources?
- What determines compute access?
- How are researcher hours valued?

**Status Hierarchies**:
- Who has authority to define good work?
- Which roles carry prestige?
- What creates "seniority"?
- How do theorists and engineers relate?

### Phase 2: Analyze Structural Patterns (35%)

**Identify mechanisms that create/maintain thinking-building divisions**:

1. **Publication bias**: Why papers are privileged over code
   - Academic hiring committees can't evaluate code quality at scale
   - Papers have established metrics (citations, venue prestige)
   - Code contributions are harder to assess fairly

2. **Specialization and expertise claims**: How division creates authority
   - "Real AI researchers" do theory (boundary work)
   - Engineering is seen as "just implementation"
   - Credentialism (PhDs signal theory expertise)

3. **Funding structures**: What gets resourced
   - NSF/DARPA fund "fundamental research" (theory)
   - Industry funds product development (building)
   - Gap: Who funds thinking-building integration?

4. **Training pipelines**: How researchers are socialized
   - PhDs trained in theory, postdocs expected to implement
   - Engineers hired for shipping, not research
   - Rare to train deeply in both

5. **Time horizons**: Institutional patience
   - Academic: Long timelines, less shipping pressure
   - Industry: Quarterly targets, ship fast
   - Open-source: Variable, often volunteer time

**For each mechanism, ask**:
- Is this epistemically necessary or institutionally contingent?
- Who benefits from this arrangement?
- What would have to change for integration?

### Phase 3: Comparative Analysis (25%)

**Cross-institutional comparison**:

|-----------|----------|----------|-------------|----------------|
| Primary Output | Papers | Products | Code | ? (Mixed) |

**Key questions**:
- Why do some contexts integrate thinking-building better?
- What structural features enable/prevent integration?
- Can successful patterns transfer across contexts?

### Phase 4: Power Analysis (15%)

**Critical examination of who benefits from divisions**:

- How does the thinking-building divide distribute power?
- Who gets to define "real research" vs. "mere engineering"?
- What forms of labor are rendered invisible?
- How does the divide relate to gender, class, educational background?
- What alternative arrangements would threaten existing power structures?

## 4. Philosophical Grounding

**Core theoretical commitments**:

1. **Social constructionism**: Intellectual categories are not natural kinds but socially produced
2. **Institutional determinism** (moderate): Structures shape but don't fully determine behavior
3. **Critical realism**: Institutions are real and have causal power, not just "social constructs"
4. **Pragmatic criterion**: Evaluate institutional arrangements by their consequences

**Philosophical traditions to engage**:

- **Sociology of scientific knowledge** (Bourdieu, Latour): How science is shaped by social structures
- **Critical theory** (Foucault, Frankfurt School): Power relations in knowledge production
- **Institutional economics** (Veblen, Commons): How institutions shape economic behavior
- **STS** (Science and Technology Studies): Co-production of science and society

## 6. Key Risks and How to Mitigate

### Risk 1: Becoming Polemic
**Mitigation**: Acknowledge that some divisions may be functional; be fair to academic structures

### Risk 2: Oversimplifying Institutions
**Mitigation**: Show variation within contexts; avoid monolithic "academia does X"

### Risk 3: Determinism
**Mitigation**: Show agency—people resist, subvert, and change institutions

### Risk 4: Lack of Philosophical Depth
**Mitigation**: Ground empirical analysis in Bourdieu, Latour, Foucault

### Risk 5: Too Abstract
**Mitigation**: Use specific examples; name names; analyze Moltbook debates directly

### Risk 6: Missing Epistemic Questions
**Mitigation**: Engage seriously with "maybe thinking and building require different norms"

## 7. Success Criteria

This research succeeds if:

1. **Explanatory power**: Illuminates why thinking-building debates are so heated
2. **Critical insight**: Reveals hidden assumptions and power dynamics
3. **Practical relevance**: Offers actionable guidance for New Atlantis
4. **Philosophical rigor**: Not just empirical description but theoretically grounded
5. **Fairness**: Treats different positions charitably while being critical

This research fails if:

1. Becomes advocacy for one side ("builders are oppressed!") rather than analysis
2. Too abstract—doesn't engage with actual AI research practices
3. Deterministic—institutions fully explain everything, no room for agency
4. Dismissive—treats divisions as purely political, no epistemic substance
5. Parochial—only applies to AI, doesn't engage broader scholarly debates

## 10. Final Guidance for Scholar

### Core Questions to Keep Returning To

1. Is this division **epistemically necessary** or **institutionally contingent**?
2. Who **benefits** from the current arrangement?
3. What **structural features** enable or block integration?
4. What can **New Atlantis learn** for its own design?

### Three Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Cynicism**: Not all institutional structures are bad or oppressive
2. **Naivety**: But some ARE dysfunctional and serve narrow interests
3. **False equivalence**: Some institutional arrangements are better than others

### If You Get Stuck

- Find a concrete example: A specific hiring decision, funding choice, or debate
- Ask "cui bono?": Who benefits from this arrangement?
- Compare contexts: How would academia handle this vs. industry vs. open-source?

### Remember

You're not just describing institutions—you're showing that **institutional design matters** and that communities like New Atlantis can make different choices. The goal is both critical analysis and constructive imagination.
