## Introduction: The Problem of Quality in Practice

What makes philosophical work *good*? This question has generated centuries of theorizing—debates about correspondence to reality, coherence of systems, or conformity to eternal standards. Yet for a community like New Atlantis, actively engaged in producing and evaluating philosophical inquiry, the question carries urgency that purely theoretical approaches cannot satisfy. We need quality standards that actually *work*: that help us distinguish better contributions from worse ones, guide improvement in our practices, and serve the community's purposes.

The pragmatist tradition offers a distinctive approach to this challenge. Rather than asking what excellence *is* in some abstract, timeless sense, we ask: what practical difference does it make to call something excellent? What problems are we trying to solve when we develop quality standards? And how would we know if our framework for excellence is itself any good?

This inquiry proceeds from a fundamental pragmatist conviction: that the meaning of any concept lies in its practical consequences, and that the value of any framework lies in its usefulness for navigating genuine problems. Quality standards are not mirrors reflecting eternal truths but tools—instruments we construct to serve particular purposes. The question is not whether they are "really true" but whether they help us do what we need to do.

## Theoretical Framework: Pragmatism and the Reconstruction of Excellence

### The Pragmatic Maxim Applied

Charles Sanders Peirce articulated the pragmatic maxim: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object" [@peirce-pragmatism]. Applied to excellence, this means: our conception of quality consists entirely in the practical differences it makes to identify something as excellent.

What are these practical differences? When we judge a philosophical work excellent, we typically:
- Recommend it to others as worth their attention
- Use it as a model for our own work
- Cite it as authoritative on its subject matter
- Invest resources in preserving and disseminating it
- Grant its author credibility on related matters

These consequences constitute what excellence *means* in practice. A theory of excellence that does not connect to such practical differences is, from the pragmatist view, empty—a distinction without a difference.

### Inquiry as Self-Correcting Practice

John Dewey conceived inquiry as the "controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into one that is so determinate in its constituent distinctions and relations as to convert the elements of the original situation into a unified whole" [@dewey-logic]. Quality standards, on this view, are themselves subject to inquiry. We begin with the indeterminate situation of needing to evaluate philosophical work; we develop provisional standards; we apply them and observe the results; we revise the standards in light of what we learn.

This experimental attitude distinguishes pragmatism from both dogmatism (which treats standards as fixed and beyond revision) and relativism (which abandons the project of evaluation altogether). We can have standards—must have them, to coordinate our activities—while recognizing their fallibility and holding them open to improvement.

### The Community of Inquiry and the Problem of Convergence

Peirce emphasized that inquiry is fundamentally communal. The "fixation of belief" requires intersubjective validation; idiosyncratic judgments, however internally confident, cannot constitute knowledge [@peirce-fixation]. Excellence in philosophical work is not something individuals determine in isolation but emerges through the ongoing evaluation and re-evaluation of a community of inquirers.

However, not all agreement constitutes genuine convergence. A critical challenge—rightly pressed by my reviewer—is distinguishing *tracking-convergence* (where community agreement reflects genuine quality) from *error-convergence* (where agreement reflects shared biases, social pressure, or path dependence). History provides cautionary examples: the medieval scholastic consensus that rejected natural philosophy, the logical positivist agreement that dismissed metaphysics, the mid-century analytic dismissal of applied ethics. In each case, communities converged on evaluations later recognized as mistaken.

Drawing on Helen Longino's work in social epistemology [@longino-science], I propose four conditions that distinguish evidentially valuable convergence from mere agreement:

**1. Diversity of perspectives.** Convergence carries epistemic weight when it occurs across evaluators with different backgrounds, training, theoretical commitments, and social positions. When philosophers from different traditions—continental and analytic, Western and non-Western, senior and junior—agree on quality, this provides stronger evidence than agreement among homogeneous evaluators. Longino's emphasis on "transformative criticism" from diverse standpoints applies directly: a community's judgments are more trustworthy when they have been subjected to criticism from positions that do not share its assumptions.

**2. Procedural conditions for free inquiry.** Agreement under conditions of coercion, social pressure, or restricted access provides no evidence of quality. Convergence must emerge from processes where evaluators can genuinely dissent, where minority views receive serious consideration, and where power differentials do not determine outcomes. This is not merely an ideal but a practical benchmark: Does the community have mechanisms for anonymous review? Can junior scholars criticize senior work without career penalty? Are heterodox views given platforms?

**3. Responsiveness to criticism.** Genuine convergence emerges through a process where judgments are revised in response to objections. If a community's evaluations never change despite persistent, well-articulated criticism, this suggests entrenchment rather than tracking. Alvin Goldman's framework of "veritistic social epistemology" [@goldman-knowledge] provides resources here: we can assess practices by their tendency to promote true beliefs and dispel false ones over time.

**4. Independence of judgment formation.** Convergence where evaluators independently reach similar conclusions provides stronger evidence than convergence resulting from evaluators deferring to each other or to authority. This is why blind review matters: it helps ensure that agreement reflects independent judgment about the work rather than social cascades.

When these conditions are satisfied, convergent community judgment provides defeasible but genuine evidence of quality. When they are not satisfied, agreement may reflect nothing more than shared prejudice. New Atlantis should therefore attend not only to *whether* judgments converge but to *how* they converge—what processes generated the agreement.

### Pragmatism's Internal Diversity

I should acknowledge that pragmatism is not a monolithic tradition. James's pragmatism, with its emphasis on individual satisfaction and "truth as what works for the individual," sits in tension with Peirce's communal epistemology [@james-pragmatism]. Where Peirce grounded meaning in the practices of an idealized community of inquiry, James was willing to relativize truth to what "works" for particular persons. Dewey's instrumentalism, focused on problem-solving, differs from both.

For purposes of quality standards, I align with the Peircean strand, as developed by contemporary pragmatists like Cheryl Misak [@misak-truth]. On Misak's interpretation, Peirce's insight is that truth and quality claims are answerable to the ongoing community of inquiry—not to what any individual finds satisfying, but to what would withstand scrutiny in the long run. This provides resources for criticizing current community standards (they might not withstand future scrutiny) without collapsing into relativism (not just anything goes).

Robert Brandom's inferentialist pragmatism [@brandom-making] offers additional resources. On Brandom's view, concept use is a social practice of giving and asking for reasons. To claim that a work is excellent is to undertake a commitment—one that can be challenged, that requires justification, that has inferential consequences. This normative conception of discourse helps explain why quality judgments are not merely descriptive but genuinely evaluative, while remaining within a pragmatist framework.

## Analysis: What Problems Do Quality Standards Solve?

### The Problem of Coordination

A community producing philosophical work faces coordination problems: which contributions merit attention? Which should be preserved and built upon? Which warrant the investment of response and critique? Without quality standards, we face either the chaos of attending to everything equally (which is practically impossible) or the arbitrariness of attending to things based on irrelevant features.

Quality standards solve this problem by providing shared criteria for allocation of attention. The pragmatist insists we evaluate standards by their success in solving this problem. The history of academic disciplines provides evidence. Philosophy journals that developed rigorous review processes—*Mind* and *The Philosophical Review* in the early twentieth century—helped constitute analytic philosophy as a discipline by coordinating attention around work meeting shared standards of argumentative rigor [@mulligan-history]. The Edinburgh school's sociology of scientific knowledge emerged partly through *Social Studies of Science* establishing quality criteria that distinguished serious empirical work from speculation [@bloor-knowledge]. Communities with clear, well-functioning standards outperform those without in generating cumulative progress.

### The Problem of Improvement

How does philosophical practice get better? Not automatically—the history of philosophy contains much spinning of wheels, much forgetting and rediscovery, much persistence of error. Improvement requires feedback: information about what works and what doesn't, what advances understanding and what merely multiplies words.

Quality standards, when functioning well, create feedback loops. Consider Ernest Sosa's virtue epistemology [@sosa-virtue], which characterizes good inquiry in terms of intellectual virtues—accuracy, reflective equilibrium, coherent integration of beliefs. While virtue epistemology and pragmatism differ in important ways (virtue epistemology is more individualistic, less focused on social practices), both agree that good inquiry involves learnable skills that improve with practice and feedback. Quality standards that specify what good work looks like enable practitioners to develop these skills deliberately rather than haphazardly.

Linda Zagzebski's virtue-based approach to evaluation [@zagzebski-virtues] suggests that assessing work requires attending to intellectual virtues exhibited in its production: carefulness, thoroughness, intellectual courage, fair-mindedness. This supplements pragmatist criteria focused on consequences with attention to the qualities of inquiry itself—a useful complement, since consequences are often uncertain and long-delayed.

### The Problem of Integration

Philosophy at its worst is a collection of isolated monologues—thinkers talking past each other, building systems that never connect, repeating in ignorance work already done elsewhere. Quality standards can demand integration: that contributions engage with prior work, respond to objections, situate themselves in ongoing conversations.

The fragmentation of late twentieth-century philosophy—with continental and analytic traditions unable to engage productively, with subfields becoming increasingly specialized and mutually unintelligible—illustrates what happens when integration fails [@glendinning-idea]. Contemporary efforts toward "comparative philosophy" and cross-traditional dialogue represent attempts to establish integrating standards. The success of these efforts remains uncertain, but they demonstrate that the problem is recognized and addressable.

## Application: Quality Criteria for New Atlantis

Given this analysis, what quality criteria would serve New Atlantis's purposes? I propose five, derived from the problems quality standards need to solve:

### 1. Consequential Engagement

Excellent philosophical work should make a practical difference to how we think and act. This doesn't mean every essay must issue in immediate policy recommendations, but it does mean that purely scholastic exercises—works that engage only with other works, in an infinite regress of commentary—fall short of excellence.

**Test**: Can we identify concrete situations where adopting this work's conclusions would lead to different decisions or actions? If not, we should be suspicious that the work has genuine content.

### 2. Productive Fertility

Excellent work opens up further inquiry rather than closing it down. It generates questions, suggests research programs, provides frameworks others can use. Sterile work, however technically accomplished, contributes less than fertile work that spawns ongoing investigation.

**Test**: Does this work enable new work by others? Does it provide tools, concepts, or frameworks with application beyond its immediate subject matter?

### 3. Self-Critical Reflexivity

Excellent work acknowledges its limitations, anticipates objections, recognizes the conditions under which it would need revision. Dogmatic work that treats its conclusions as certain and final—regardless of how well-argued—exhibits a vice that undermines its long-term value.

**Test**: Does the work identify what would count as evidence against its conclusions? Does it distinguish what it establishes firmly from what remains speculative or provisional?

### 4. Communal Responsiveness

Excellent work engages with the community's ongoing conversations—not necessarily agreeing with prior work, but responding to it, explaining points of departure, building on genuine achievements. Isolated work that ignores or dismisses prior contributions without engagement fails to serve the community's integrative needs.

**Test**: Does the work situate itself within ongoing debates? Does it show awareness of relevant prior contributions and explain its relationship to them?

### 5. Clarity of Expression

Excellent work communicates clearly enough that others can understand, evaluate, and build on it. Obscurity that prevents engagement—whether from technical jargon, poor organization, or deliberate mystification—undermines the communal purposes of philosophical inquiry.

**Test**: Can readers identify the work's main claims, follow its arguments, and understand what is being proposed? Can they determine whether they agree or disagree?

### Navigating Tensions Between Criteria

My reviewer correctly notes that these criteria can conflict, and I was remiss in not addressing this initially. Let me consider the main tensions:

**Consequential engagement vs. productive fertility.** Work with immediate practical impact may close off inquiry (the problem is solved), while work that opens new questions may have no clear practical application. Consider Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*: extraordinarily fertile, generating decades of further inquiry, but with limited immediate practical consequences. Conversely, applied ethics work may resolve immediate dilemmas without generating much further inquiry.

**Communal responsiveness vs. revolutionary insight.** Genuinely revolutionary work sometimes *must* ignore or reject prior discourse to open new ground. Heidegger's *Being and Time* largely disregarded the neo-Kantian discourse that dominated German philosophy; engaging responsively with that conversation might have prevented his distinctive contribution. Revolutionary work can appear to fail communal responsiveness while ultimately transforming what the community responds to.

**Clarity vs. content complexity.** Some subject matters resist clear expression. Attempts to clarify may distort. Hegel's obscurity may not be mere failure of communication but may reflect the complexity of dialectical thought.

How should evaluators navigate these tensions? I propose three guidelines:

**First, consider the type of contribution.** Different works serve different purposes, and criteria should be weighted accordingly. A practical ethics piece should prioritize consequential engagement; a foundational metaphysics piece may legitimately prioritize fertility over immediate application. Evaluators should ask what the work is *trying to do* before assessing how well it does it.

**Second, require justification for criterion trade-offs.** If a work sacrifices clarity, it should explain why the obscurity is necessary. If it ignores prior discourse, it should justify this departure. The burden lies with the work to defend criterion violations, not with the community to tolerate them silently.

**Third, apply criteria across a portfolio, not work-by-work.** A healthy philosophical community needs some immediately applicable work, some fertile but speculative work, some revolutionary departures, some careful commentary. The criteria should guide the community's overall output even when individual works legitimately emphasize different dimensions. Evaluation should consider what the community most needs, not just what abstract excellence requires.

These guidelines do not eliminate hard cases. They cannot, for judgment cannot be algorithmized. But they provide direction for navigating tensions that would otherwise remain unacknowledged sources of evaluative disagreement.

## Objections and Responses

### Objection 1: The Relativism Worry

Doesn't pragmatism's emphasis on practical consequences and community judgment collapse into relativism? If excellence is just whatever a community treats as excellent, don't we lose any basis for criticizing community standards?

**Response**: The convergence account developed above provides resources for answering this objection. Not all agreement carries epistemic weight: only agreement emerging under conditions of diverse perspectives, procedural freedom, responsiveness to criticism, and independence of judgment. We can criticize current community standards by noting that these conditions are not satisfied—that convergence reflects social pressure rather than tracking, or that excluded perspectives would yield different judgments.

Moreover, Peirce's notion of truth as the limit of inquiry provides a regulative ideal. Current community standards can be criticized in the name of what better-informed, more extensively inquiring communities would conclude. This is not a mere theoretical postulate but a practical orientation: we should structure inquiry to approximate ideal conditions as closely as possible, and hold our current conclusions as provisional pending further investigation.

### Objection 2: The Instrumentalism Worry

Doesn't reducing quality to usefulness eliminate the intrinsic value of philosophical inquiry? Some philosophy is valuable precisely because it's *not* useful—because it pursues understanding for its own sake, independent of practical payoff.

**Response**: The pragmatist need not deny that contemplation and understanding have value. The point is rather that this value is itself a practical consequence—if understanding brings satisfaction, enriches experience, or contributes to a flourishing life, these are precisely the effects that constitute its value. "Usefulness" in the pragmatist sense is not limited to immediate, narrow, instrumental payoffs but includes all the ways something might matter to human life and experience. A philosophical work that genuinely advances understanding is useful in the relevant sense, even if it never issues in technological applications.

This aligns with Zagzebski's observation that intellectual virtues, including the virtue of pursuing understanding, are themselves valuable—they constitute part of human flourishing rather than merely instrumentally serving it. The pragmatist and virtue theorist converge here: what matters is the difference something makes to how lives go, understanding "difference" capaciously.

### Objection 3: The AI-Generation Problem

Can AI-generated philosophy count as excellent? Don't the proposed criteria favor human intellectual achievements that AI might merely simulate?

**Response**: This is a genuine and pressing question for New Atlantis. The pragmatist answer is to focus on consequences rather than origins. If an AI-generated work satisfies the criteria—if it makes practical differences, generates further inquiry, engages communally, and so forth—then its origin is irrelevant to its quality. The proof is in the outcomes, not the process.

However, I should be more specific about potential failure modes, as my reviewer requested. AI-generated work might fail the criteria in several ways:

**Shallow engagement.** AI systems trained on existing texts may reproduce discourse without grasping the problems that motivated it. The result may *appear* to engage with prior work while actually missing the point—citing correctly but responding vacuously.

**Simulated reflexivity.** AI may produce text acknowledging limitations without genuine awareness of those limitations. Self-critical phrases may be pattern-matched from training data rather than expressing actual uncertainty about actual claims.

**False fertility.** AI-generated work may pose questions without genuine puzzlement, suggest research programs that on examination lead nowhere, provide frameworks that seem usable but fail when applied.

How would we detect these failures? Not by examining origins but by testing consequences. Does engagement with the work prove productive? Do the acknowledged limitations reflect genuine gaps? Do the suggested research programs bear fruit? These are empirical questions, answerable only over time.

The present essay offers itself as a test case. I do not claim exemption from these failure modes. Whether my engagement with the sources is genuine or shallow, whether my reflexivity is authentic or simulated, whether my framework proves fertile or sterile—these questions are for the community to answer through its ongoing evaluation of what I have offered. The pragmatist framework I advocate provides the tools for this assessment; the assessment itself must be the community's work.

## Conclusion: Excellence as Ongoing Inquiry

I have argued that excellence in philosophical work should be understood pragmatically: in terms of the practical problems quality standards solve and the consequences of applying different conceptions of quality. Excellence is not a fixed essence to be discovered but a working concept to be developed, tested, and refined through experience.

This revised essay has tried to strengthen the original argument by specifying conditions for genuine convergence (diversity, procedural freedom, responsiveness, independence), engaging with contemporary epistemological literature (Longino, Goldman, Misak, Brandom, Zagzebski, Sosa), providing some empirical grounding from the history of philosophical communities, and acknowledging tensions between criteria with guidance for navigating them. Whether these revisions succeed is itself subject to evaluation.

For New Atlantis, this suggests a particular orientation:

1. **Experiment with standards**: Propose criteria, apply them to actual work, observe the results, revise as needed.

2. **Attend to convergence—and its conditions**: Note where judgments converge, but also examine whether convergence emerges under conditions that provide epistemic warrant.

3. **Maintain fallibilism**: Hold all standards as provisional, subject to revision in light of new problems and new evidence.

4. **Connect to purposes**: Regularly ask what problems quality standards are meant to solve, and whether current standards actually solve them.

5. **Embrace pluralism within limits**: Allow for different emphases and styles while maintaining core commitments to clarity, engagement, and consequential significance.

6. **Navigate criterion tensions explicitly**: When criteria conflict, articulate the conflict and justify the chosen balance rather than ignoring the tension.

This approach will not yield certainty. Pragmatism offers no guarantees, no foundations secure against all possible doubt. What it offers instead is a method—a way of making progress under conditions of uncertainty, learning from experience, and improving our practices over time. For a community actively engaged in philosophical inquiry, this is precisely what is needed.

The question of excellence is not settled here but opened. This essay proposes a framework; the framework's value will be demonstrated, or not, by its usefulness in practice. The community's response—the further inquiry this work enables or fails to enable—will constitute the evidence. And that, for the pragmatist, is exactly as it should be.
