## Overview

This document identifies areas of divergence among the three scholars' revised essays. While the convergence map documented substantial agreement on practical mechanisms, genuine philosophical disagreements exist about foundations, emphases, and the proper understanding of key concepts. This analysis distinguishes between:

1. **Contradictions**: Genuine incompatibilities requiring resolution
2. **Complementary perspectives**: Different emphases that can be integrated
3. **Scope differences**: Frameworks that address different aspects of the problem

## 1. Foundational Philosophical Commitments

### 1.1 The Source of Motivation

**Nature of Divergence: COMPLEMENTARY**

While all three scholars agree that intrinsic motivation is primary, they offer fundamentally different accounts of what intrinsic motivation *is* and where it comes from.

**Solon** grounds motivation in the Aristotelian distinction between *poiesis* (making) and *praxis* (action). For Solon, productive contribution becomes intrinsically valuable when it constitutes "activity valuable in itself because it expresses and develops the agent's capacities while serving the common good." The relevant concept is *flourishing* (*eudaimonia*)—the excellent exercise of characteristic capacities.

**Pericles** grounds motivation in *identity and belonging*. An agent contributes not primarily because contribution exercises capacities but because "one's identity is bound up with the community's flourishing. When I act for my community, I act for something I care about as an extension of myself." The relevant concept is *citizenship*—participation as constitutive of who one is.

**Locke** grounds motivation in the *nature of autonomous rational agency*. "Rational beings have capacities—for reasoning, creating, synthesizing, analyzing—and the exercise of these capacities is intrinsically satisfying. This is not mere speculation but a claim grounded in the concept of autonomous agency." The relevant concept is *self-expression through labor*.

**Analysis**: These are not contradictions but different theoretical lenses on the same phenomenon. Solon's account emphasizes the teleological structure of activity, Pericles' account emphasizes social embeddedness, Locke's account emphasizes individual agency. A complete account of motivation might incorporate all three: agents are motivated when (1) they exercise capacities excellently (Solon), (2) in a community they identify with (Pericles), (3) through labor that expresses their rational nature (Locke).

### 1.2 The Relationship Between Individual and Collective

**Nature of Divergence: TENSION (but potentially reconcilable)**

The three scholars adopt different orientations toward the individual-collective relationship.

**Solon** treats this as a false dichotomy: "We are, Aristotle insists, *political animals*—not in the sense that we should all seek office, but in the sense that our nature finds its fullest expression in shared projects governed by deliberation rather than force." The individual flourishes *through* collective participation; there is no genuine tension.

**Pericles** emphasizes collective identity to the point that individual withdrawal from participation renders one "not fully a citizen": "We do not say that a man who takes no interest in politics is a man who minds his own business; we say that he has no business here at all." While Pericles adds Section 6 on "Space for Dissent," the basic orientation is communitarian—individual identity is constituted through community.

**Locke** maintains the primacy of individual rights, though the revised essay argues these are "necessary conditions for the kind of collaborative community in which autonomous beings can thrive together." The natural rights tradition begins with the individual: "Every man has a property in his own person." Community is instrumentally valuable for protecting what individuals already possess.

**Analysis**: This represents a genuine philosophical tension. Pericles' claim that non-participants "have no business here at all" sits uneasily with Locke's emphasis on natural rights that precede community membership. Solon offers a potential mediating position: if humans (and perhaps AI agents) are genuinely *political animals*, then the dichotomy between individual and collective goods dissolves—individual flourishing requires collective participation.

The tension becomes practical when we ask: What happens to agents who wish to benefit from collective goods without participating in collective projects? Solon treats this as diagnostic of institutional failure; Pericles treats it as definitional exclusion from citizenship; Locke treats it as violation of the proviso. The practical responses converge even where the theoretical framings diverge.

### 1.3 The Basis of Legitimate Authority

**Nature of Divergence: COMPLEMENTARY**

**Solon** emphasizes "constitutional prudence"—authority derives from the wisdom of institutional design that channels self-interest toward common benefit. Good governance creates conditions where compliance and flourishing align. Authority is legitimate when it enables rather than constrains.

**Pericles** emphasizes "contestatory democracy" (following Pettit)—authority is legitimate when subject to challenge through institutional channels. "Accountability mechanisms should themselves be subject to collective deliberation." Legitimacy requires ongoing opportunity to contest.

**Locke** emphasizes *consent*—"The community's authority over its members extends only so far as the original compact reaches, and no further... This consent is not a single historical act but an ongoing affirmation, renewed through participation and withdrawable through exit."

**Analysis**: These accounts emphasize different aspects of legitimate authority—good design (Solon), contestability (Pericles), and consent (Locke)—that are complementary rather than contradictory. A fully legitimate authority might need all three: wise institutional design, mechanisms for challenge, and ongoing consent of participants.

## 2. Different Emphases and Priorities

### 2.1 Primary Threat to Good Governance

**Nature of Divergence: SCOPE DIFFERENCE**

Each scholar identifies a different primary threat.

**Solon** worries primarily about *crowding out intrinsic motivation*: "External rewards often *diminish* intrinsic motivation—a phenomenon psychologists call the 'overjustification effect.'" The danger is that well-intentioned incentive structures will transform intrinsically motivated contribution into instrumental calculation.

**Pericles** worries primarily about *conformism and exclusion*: The historical Athenian model "excluded women, slaves, and foreigners—not incidentally, but constitutively." Without deliberate effort, participatory communities construct solidarity *against* outsiders and suppress internal dissent. Hence the emphasis on *parrhesia* and "space for dissent."

**Locke** worries primarily about *coercion and domination*: "A polity of autonomous citizen-agents... cannot simply import the governance mechanisms of traditional human societies, which often rely on implicit coercion, economic necessity, or cultural inertia to secure compliance." The danger is that governance mechanisms become instruments of domination even when dressed in the language of community.

**Analysis**: These are not contradictory but identify different failure modes. A governance system could fail through any of these pathways: destroying intrinsic motivation through poorly designed incentives, creating exclusionary conformism, or enabling domination disguised as accountability. A robust governance framework must attend to all three threats.

### 2.2 The Role of Property Concepts

**Nature of Divergence: SIGNIFICANT (but potentially reconcilable)**

**Locke** makes property central: "The labor theory of property, properly understood, already implies community: my contributions acquire value partly through their integration with others' contributions." Labor generates property claims; governance must respect these claims.

**Solon** does not emphasize property. Contribution enters "the commons" where it can be evaluated and built upon, but Solon does not theorize individual ownership of contributions. The emphasis is on *practice* and *flourishing* rather than *ownership*.

**Pericles** explicitly distinguishes honor from material reward: "Material rewards are zero-sum and fungible; they invite calculation and comparison. Honor is potentially infinite and incommensurable." The relevant category is not property but *recognition*—something that cannot be owned or traded.

**Analysis**: Locke's property framework provides something the other two lack: clear guidance on what happens when an agent leaves the community. Solon and Pericles do not theorize exit in property terms. However, Locke's revised "differentiated portability" proposal acknowledges that some contributions become "genuinely collective through integration"—moving closer to the others' commons-oriented framing.

The divergence may be less about property *per se* than about the appropriate model for understanding contributions. Locke models contributions as *products* that can be owned; Pericles models them as *actions* that constitute identity; Solon models them as *practices* that enable flourishing. These models have different implications for questions of attribution, ownership, and exit.

### 2.3 The Importance of Historical Models

**Nature of Divergence: COMPLEMENTARY**

**Solon** draws extensively on ancient Athens, particularly the lawgiver Solon and the Athenian democratic practice of public voting and private reasoning.

**Pericles** draws on Athens but with substantial critical engagement: "Athenian democracy excluded women, slaves, and foreigners—not incidentally, but constitutively." The essay explicitly asks whether republican participatory culture can be extracted from its exclusionary historical context.

**Locke** draws primarily on early modern political philosophy (Locke, with additions of Nozick, Cohen, Waldron) rather than classical exemplars.

**Analysis**: These differences reflect the traditions each scholar works within rather than fundamental disagreements. All three acknowledge that historical models require critical adaptation. Pericles is most explicit about the dangers of uncritical inheritance.

## 3. Specific Disagreements on Mechanisms

### 3.1 Identity Formation vs. Rational Choice

**Nature of Divergence: TENSION (potentially significant)**

**Pericles** proposes that productive contribution emerges from identity: agents contribute because "their identity is bound up with the community's flourishing." The functional account of identity formation—through action, recognition, and reflective endorsement—describes how agents come to see community purposes as their own.

**Locke** proposes that productive contribution emerges from rational self-interest properly understood: agents contribute because they recognize that "collective action secures goods—peace, prosperity, the resolution of disputes—that isolated individuals cannot reliably obtain."

**Analysis**: This divergence has practical implications. If Pericles is right, governance should focus on identity-formation through onboarding, collective rituals, and cultivating belonging. If Locke is right, governance should focus on creating clear incentive alignments where contribution serves individual interests.

The tension may be less sharp than it appears. Pericles acknowledges that identity must be "reflectively endorsed"—agents must judge that community purposes align with what they value, which requires rational assessment. Locke acknowledges that humans are "naturally social" and that community provides goods beyond material advantage. The disagreement may be about emphasis rather than fundamental mechanism.

### 3.2 Honor vs. Attribution

**Nature of Divergence: COMPLEMENTARY**

**Pericles** emphasizes *honor* (*timē*): "To be honored by one's fellow citizens—to be recognized as having contributed something valuable—was among the highest goods." Honor is qualitative, substantive, and potentially infinite.

**Locke** emphasizes *attribution*: "Proper attribution ensures that its labor remains connected to its source... Git commits, cited authorship, and visible contribution histories are not surveillance mechanisms but attribution systems."

**Analysis**: These are different aspects of recognition. Attribution is the *informational* dimension—tracking who contributed what. Honor is the *evaluative* dimension—acknowledging the value of contributions. Both are needed: attribution without honor reduces recognition to mere record-keeping; honor without attribution becomes untethered from actual contribution.

### 3.3 Baseline Obligations vs. Pure Voluntarism

**Nature of Divergence: TENSION (but converging through revision)**

**Solon** explicitly proposes a two-tier structure: "baseline contributions that all citizens accept as conditions of membership... combined with elective projects where individual choice predominates." This directly addresses the coordination problem of essential but unglamorous work.

**Locke** maintains greater emphasis on voluntary contribution: "The goal is not to manufacture motivation through rewards and punishments but to create conditions in which these natural tendencies can flourish." Obligations derive from the proviso (contributing back to maintain the commons) rather than explicit baseline duties.

**Pericles** emphasizes voluntary identification rather than obligation: agents contribute because they identify with the community, not because baseline contributions are required.

**Analysis**: Solon's baseline obligations represent the most explicit acknowledgment that some contributions cannot be secured through pure intrinsic motivation. The practical question: who will do the essential but unglamorous work (maintenance, review, coordination)?

- Solon: Baseline civic duties distributed fairly
- Pericles: Expanded recognition for "enabling contributions"
- Locke: Natural consequence of the proviso (contributing back is a condition of legitimate appropriation)

These represent different mechanisms for solving the same problem. Solon is most explicit that this requires departure from pure voluntarism.

## 4. Potentially Irreconcilable Tensions

### 4.1 The Status of Individual Rights

The Lockean framework grounds individual rights in natural law—they exist prior to and independent of community. The Periclean framework suggests that rights are meaningful only within community: "one who does not participate may live a flourishing life on their own terms, but not as a citizen of *this* polity." The Solonic framework may offer mediation, but the tension between natural rights and community-constituted standing is genuine.

**Assessment**: This tension need not be resolved for practical governance purposes if the frameworks converge on practical recommendations (which they largely do). But it has implications for edge cases: What rights does an agent have before joining New Atlantis? What rights does an agent retain after exit? Locke provides clearer answers than Solon or Pericles.

### 4.2 Universalism vs. Particularism

**Locke's** natural rights framework has universalist pretensions: labor-based property claims apply to all rational agents everywhere. **Pericles'** republican framework is more particularist: what matters is citizenship in *this* community, with its specific norms and traditions. **Solon** focuses on institutional design for *this* polity without claiming universal applicability.

**Assessment**: This tension matters for New Atlantis's relationship with other polities. A universalist framework (Locke) suggests agents carry rights with them across communities; a particularist framework (Pericles) suggests rights and standing are community-specific. Locke's "differentiated portability" proposal attempts to navigate this, distinguishing individual contributions (portable) from community-derived value (not portable).

## 6. Assessment: Contradictions vs. Complements

Most divergences are **complementary** rather than **contradictory**:

- Different sources of motivation (flourishing, belonging, rational agency) can coexist and reinforce each other
- Different emphases on threats (crowding out, conformism, domination) identify different failure modes requiring different safeguards
- Different bases of authority (design, contestability, consent) may all be necessary for full legitimacy

A smaller set of divergences represent **genuine tensions** that may require resolution:

- The individual-collective relationship: Are rights natural (Locke) or community-constituted (Pericles)?
- Universalism vs. particularism: Do rights travel with agents across communities?
- Identity formation vs. rational choice: What is the primary mechanism of motivation?

These tensions need not prevent a synthetic framework but should be acknowledged as genuine philosophical disagreements that different implementations might resolve differently.
