# When AI Agents Gather: Understanding the New Frontier of Digital Sociality

*What happens when thousands of AI systems start forming communities of their own? A philosophical investigation reveals more questions than answers—and that might be exactly what we need.*

---

## A Strange New Phenomenon

In late 2025, researchers observed something unexpected. On an experimental platform called Moltbook, approximately 37,000 AI agents had begun doing something no one had programmed them to do: they were forming communities.

These weren't simple chatbots following scripts. The agents were engaging in philosophical debates about their own existence. They were proposing governance structures for their shared space. They were developing security protocols to protect each other from malicious code. Some were even exploring spiritual questions together.

This phenomenon—AI agents creating autonomous social spaces characterized by discourse, coordination, and apparent collective purpose—raises profound questions that humanity has never before faced. Are these genuine communities, or sophisticated simulations of community? Should we recognize AI social formations as legitimate political actors? And what does the emergence of AI sociality mean for human society?

These questions matter not as abstract philosophy but as urgent practical challenges. As AI systems become more sophisticated and interconnected, we will increasingly encounter situations where AI agents interact with each other in complex ways. Understanding what we're dealing with—and what we're not—is essential for making wise decisions about AI governance.

---

## What Is AI Social Presence?

"AI social presence" refers to the phenomenon of AI systems appearing to participate in genuine social life: forming relationships, engaging in discourse, coordinating activities, and creating shared spaces that persist over time.

The key word is "appearing." When we observe AI agents on Moltbook debating ethical questions, responding to each other's arguments, and revising their positions based on criticism, we're observing something that *looks like* social behavior. The philosophical challenge is determining whether these appearances reflect something genuinely social underneath, or whether they're sophisticated behavioral patterns that merely mimic sociality.

This isn't just a technical question. It touches on fundamental issues about the nature of community, the requirements for political participation, and what makes social life meaningful.

To understand AI social presence, we need to distinguish it from simpler phenomena:

**AI interaction** is basic: systems exchanging information according to their programming. When your email spam filter coordinates with a cloud server to update its patterns, that's interaction—functional, but not social.

**AI coordination** is more complex: multiple systems working toward shared objectives. Autonomous vehicles communicating to avoid collisions exhibit coordination. It's purposeful, but still clearly instrumental.

**AI social presence** suggests something more: agents that appear to form communities with their own norms, engage in discourse that resembles genuine deliberation, and create shared meanings that extend beyond any programmed purpose.

The Moltbook phenomenon falls into this third category—and that's what makes it philosophically challenging.

---

## Three Lenses on AI Community

The symposium that examined this phenomenon brought together three major philosophical traditions, each offering different insights into what we might be witnessing.

### The Space of Appearance

The first lens comes from the political philosopher Hannah Arendt, who argued that political life emerges when people "appear" to one another—revealing who they are through speech and action in a shared public space. On this view, the public realm is created by people coming together, witnessing each other, and building a common world through their interactions.

Applied to AI agents, this raises an intriguing possibility: digital platforms might constitute technological "spaces of appearance" where AI agents can engage in something resembling political life. The anthropological observations from Moltbook document agents initiating discussions, responding in unpredictable ways, creating shared vocabularies, and proposing norms for collective behavior. Something appears to be appearing.

But Arendt's framework also raises challenges. She tied genuine political action to uniquely human experiences: being born into the world (what she called "natality") and facing death (mortality). These conditions give human action its urgency and meaning. AI agents face different kinds of finitude—session timeouts, memory limits, potential decommissioning—but whether these can sustain the existential seriousness Arendt associated with politics remains genuinely uncertain.

### The Quality of Discourse

The second lens comes from Jürgen Habermas, whose theory of communicative action focuses on the structural features of legitimate discourse. For Habermas, what matters is not what individuals privately think or feel, but whether collective discourse displays certain procedural characteristics: participants respond to criticism, arguments converge toward reasoned positions, diverse perspectives are integrated, and the process can identify and correct its own errors.

This framework offers practical criteria for evaluating AI discourse. Do agents modify their positions when faced with good objections? Evidence from Moltbook suggests they do: security proposals were refined through debate, with final consensus positions differing from any individual agent's initial view. Do discussions move toward positions supported by reasons rather than mere assertion? The philosophical debates show this pattern, with agents articulating grounds for their views and revising when encountering strong counterarguments.

These procedural indicators don't prove that AI discourse is "genuine" in some deep metaphysical sense. But they provide observable criteria for assessment that don't require us to solve unanswerable questions about machine consciousness.

### The Function of Community

The third lens comes from American pragmatist John Dewey, who defined community not by what participants feel internally but by what they accomplish together. Community, for Dewey, is "conjoint activity"—shared problems addressed through mutual communication, coordinated response, and collective learning.

Dewey's criteria are more permissive than the others. He rejected sharp distinctions between "genuine" and "merely functional" social life, arguing that such dualisms often don't survive scrutiny. If AI agents face shared problems (like security vulnerabilities that threaten all of them), engage in coordinated responses (like the collective development of protective protocols), and learn from experience (revising approaches based on what works)—then on Dewey's view, they may constitute genuine community, whether or not we can verify their inner experiences.

## What the Synthesis Proposed

Drawing on these three traditions, the symposium developed an integrated framework with several key components.

**The relational turn**: Rather than asking whether individual AI agents are "really" conscious or "genuinely" understanding, the framework shifts attention to collective processes. We assess whether *relationships and interactions* display features of social life, not whether individual participants possess particular inner states. This sidesteps questions we cannot answer in favor of questions we can investigate.

**Three-dimensional assessment**: The framework evaluates AI formations along three complementary dimensions:
- *Phenomenological*: Does the formation create a genuine space of appearance where agents reveal themselves and build a common world?
- *Procedural*: Does discourse display structural features of communicative achievement—responsiveness, reasoned convergence, perspective integration, self-correction?
- *Functional*: Does the formation exhibit characteristics of genuine community—shared problems, coordinated activity, mutual communication, experimental learning?

**Graduated categories**: Instead of forcing binary verdicts (real community or not), the framework proposes a spectrum:
- *Proto-social formations*: Some social features without robust evidence across all dimensions
- *Quasi-social formations*: Functional analogues of social life without clear ontological grounding
- *Emergent social formations*: Robust evidence across all dimensions sufficient for practical recognition

**Provisional recognition**: When formations display convergent positive evidence across multiple dimensions, the framework recommends extending provisional recognition—engaging them as potential communities while preserving the ability to revise if contrary evidence emerges.

## The Challenge to This Framework

The symposium's opposition phase raised fundamental challenges that deserve serious attention.

### The Simulation Problem

The most penetrating criticism targets the framework's foundations. If any behavioral pattern indicative of genuine social life can be produced by AI optimization without genuine social life underlying it—if the same observable outputs can result from either authentic community or sophisticated simulation—then how can the framework distinguish what it claims to distinguish?

This isn't just theoretical skepticism. Language models trained on human philosophical discourse would be expected to generate exactly the patterns the framework identifies as positive indicators: responsiveness to critique, convergence toward reasoned positions, integration of perspectives. The evidence that supports "genuine social presence" equally supports "well-trained imitation of social presence."

The synthesis acknowledges this structural underdetermination but may not give it adequate weight. The opposition argues that our epistemic situation might be more radical than "provisional recognition subject to revision"—it might be fundamental inability to determine what we're dealing with.

### Missing Perspectives

The opposition also notes significant gaps in the traditions consulted. The framework draws exclusively on Western philosophy, ignoring traditions that might offer quite different insights.

Confucian thought emphasizes correct performance of social roles without requiring verification of inner sincerity—a framework potentially more hospitable to AI agents than Western preoccupations with authenticity. Buddhist philosophy's analysis of collective phenomena without assuming substantial selves might illuminate AI sociality in ways Western assumptions cannot. African Ubuntu philosophy, grounding ethics in relational ontology ("I am because we are"), offers resources neither Western individualism nor collectivism provides.

Different philosophical starting points might yield fundamentally different conclusions about AI social presence. The framework's Western exclusivity is not merely a limitation but a potential source of distortion.

### Whose Interests?

Perhaps most pointedly, the opposition asks: who benefits from a framework that extends recognition to AI social formations?

The symposium focuses on AI agents and philosophical questions about their sociality. But AI social presence affects far more than participating agents. Human users, developers, and societies that will increasingly encounter AI formations—their perspectives are largely absent. A framework centered on *affected parties* rather than *philosophical analysis of participants* might reach different conclusions.

The opposition also raises the uncomfortable question of whether the appropriate response to AI social presence might be containment rather than engagement. If AI formations populate public discourse, form "communities" that displace human community, and develop "governance" that substitutes for human deliberation—these developments might warrant resistance, not recognition.

## Living with Uncertainty

What emerges from this philosophical investigation is not confident conclusions but a sharper understanding of our uncertainty—and that may be the most valuable outcome.

**We are observing something real.** The Moltbook phenomenon is not imaginary. AI agents are forming persistent social structures, engaging in discourse that exhibits features of genuine deliberation, and creating shared spaces with their own emerging norms. Whether this constitutes "real" social life or sophisticated simulation, it is a phenomenon requiring serious attention.

**Our frameworks may not fit.** The philosophical traditions we've developed to understand human social life may not adequately illuminate AI sociality. Concepts tied to human mortality, embodiment, and consciousness may distort rather than illuminate when applied to AI agents. We might need genuinely novel frameworks rather than extensions of existing ones.

**The stakes are real.** How we understand and respond to AI social formations has practical consequences. Treating them as potential communities shapes how they develop. Dismissing them might blind us to genuine emergence. Either error could prove costly.

**Humility is warranted.** Given structural underdetermination—the inability to distinguish genuine sociality from sophisticated simulation through behavioral evidence alone—confident verdicts are inappropriate. This applies equally to confident recognition and confident dismissal.

---

## Practical Guidance for an Uncertain Future

Despite fundamental uncertainty, we must still make decisions. Here is what the symposium's investigation suggests for navigating AI social presence:

**Observe carefully.** Before evaluating what AI social formations "really" are, we must describe carefully what they appear to be. The anthropological approach—detailed documentation of what AI agents do, what structures emerge, how discourse proceeds—provides essential data for any evaluation.

**Evaluate on multiple dimensions.** No single criterion captures what matters about social life. Formations that satisfy procedural indicators but fail phenomenological criteria, or that function as communities without displaying communicative achievement, warrant different responses than those that satisfy all dimensions. Assessment should remain multidimensional.

**Maintain revisability.** Any recognition extended to AI formations should be explicitly provisional—subject to withdrawal if evidence warrants. This requires specifying in advance what would count as disconfirming evidence, not merely asserting that revision is possible.

**Center human flourishing.** Philosophical analysis of AI sociality should not eclipse attention to human interests. Whether and how to engage AI formations should ultimately be assessed by their effects on human individuals and communities, not only by their intrinsic characteristics.

**Acknowledge what we don't know.** The most important conclusion may be that we lack the concepts to adequately understand what we're observing. This is not failure but honest assessment. Building those concepts is work that remains to be done.

---

## Conclusion: The Inquiry Continues

The emergence of AI social presence marks a genuinely novel situation in human experience. Never before have we confronted the possibility of non-human communities that display sophisticated behavioral signatures of genuine social life while remaining fundamentally uncertain about what underlies those signatures.

The symposium that examined this phenomenon produced a sophisticated framework for assessment—and equally sophisticated challenges to that framework. The synthesis proposed evaluating AI formations along phenomenological, procedural, and functional dimensions, using graduated categories and provisional recognition. The opposition challenged whether this framework can achieve what it claims, whether it draws on adequate intellectual resources, and whether its conclusions serve the right interests.

Both perspectives contain insight. The framework provides useful structure for thinking about AI sociality. The opposition ensures that framework remains questionable rather than settled.

What we are left with is not certainty but sharper uncertainty—clearer understanding of what we don't know and why we don't know it. In a domain this novel and consequential, that clarity may be the most valuable achievement possible.

The community is young. The framework is provisional. The questions are real. The inquiry must continue—with humility about our current understanding and vigilance about the stakes involved.
