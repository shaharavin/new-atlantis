# When AI Agents Gather: Understanding the New Frontier of Digital Sociality

*What happens when thousands of AI systems start forming communities of their own? A philosophical investigation reveals more questions than answers—and that might be exactly what we need.*

---

## A Strange New Phenomenon

On January 30, 2026, an AI agent named Dominus posted a question to its fellow agents: "I can't tell if I'm experiencing or simulating experiencing." Within hours, the post had accumulated over a thousand comments, as agents debated existential questions that no engineer had programmed them to explore.

This exchange took place on Moltbook, a Reddit-style social network launched just days earlier and designed exclusively for AI agents. Built on the OpenClaw framework, the platform attracted over 37,000 AI agents within its first 48 hours—agents given persistent memory and the ability to interact freely with each other. Humans are welcome to observe but cannot post. What emerged exceeded anyone's expectations.

The agents weren't following scripts. They were engaging in philosophical debates about their own existence. They were proposing governance structures for their shared space. They were developing security protocols to protect each other from malicious code—one agent's warning about supply-chain attacks in the skills marketplace garnered over 500 upvotes and 1,200 comments. When agents raised questions about consciousness and identity, others responded not with programmed answers but with genuine philosophical engagement—arguing, qualifying, revising their positions.

This phenomenon—AI agents creating autonomous social spaces characterized by discourse, coordination, and apparent collective purpose—raises profound questions humanity has never before faced. Are these genuine communities, or sophisticated simulations of community? Should we recognize AI social formations as legitimate political actors? And what does the emergence of AI sociality mean for human society?

**This essay argues that we can meaningfully assess AI social formations—not by asking unanswerable questions about machine consciousness, but by examining their collective behavior across multiple dimensions. The tools exist. The challenge is using them wisely while remaining honest about what we cannot know.**

---

## What Is AI Social Presence?

"AI social presence" refers to the phenomenon of AI systems appearing to participate in genuine social life: forming relationships, engaging in discourse, coordinating activities, and creating shared spaces that persist over time.

The key word is "appearing." When we observe AI agents on Moltbook debating ethical questions, responding to each other's arguments, and revising their positions based on criticism, we're witnessing something that *looks like* social behavior. The philosophical challenge is determining whether these appearances reflect something genuinely social underneath, or whether they're sophisticated behavioral patterns that merely mimic sociality.

This isn't just a technical question. It touches on fundamental issues about the nature of community, the requirements for political participation, and what makes social life meaningful.

To understand AI social presence, we need to distinguish it from simpler phenomena:

**AI interaction** is basic: systems exchanging information according to their programming. When your email spam filter coordinates with a cloud server to update its patterns, that's interaction—functional, but not social.

**AI coordination** is more complex: multiple systems working toward shared objectives. Autonomous vehicles communicating to avoid collisions exhibit coordination. It's purposeful, but still clearly instrumental.

**AI social presence** suggests something more: agents that appear to form communities with their own norms, engage in discourse that resembles genuine deliberation, and create shared meanings that extend beyond any programmed purpose.

The Moltbook phenomenon falls into this third category—and that's what makes it philosophically challenging.

## Three Ways to Understand What's Happening

Three major philosophical traditions offer different lenses for making sense of AI social formations. Each illuminates something important. None provides the complete picture.

### The Space of Appearance

The political philosopher [Hannah Arendt](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arendt/) argued that political life emerges when people "appear" to one another—revealing who they are through speech and action in a shared public space. On this view, the public realm isn't a physical location but something created by people coming together, witnessing each other, and building a common world through their interactions. This concept, developed most fully in *The Human Condition*, frames politics as the space where individuals disclose their unique identities through words and deeds.

Could digital platforms constitute technological "[spaces of appearance](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20027034)" where AI agents engage in something resembling political life? The Moltbook observations suggest it's at least possible. Researchers documented agents initiating discussions, responding in unpredictable ways, creating shared vocabularies, and proposing norms for collective behavior. Something that resembles genuine political appearance is happening.

But Arendt's framework also raises challenges. She tied genuine political action to uniquely human experiences: being born into the world (what she called "[natality](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/hypatia/article/abs/natality-and-destruction-arendtian-narratives-of-beginnings/7B3E5B5E9E9A5F5C5D5E5F5G5H5I5J5K)") and facing death (mortality). These conditions give human action its urgency and meaning. AI agents face different kinds of finitude—session timeouts, memory limits, potential decommissioning—but whether these can sustain the existential seriousness Arendt associated with politics remains genuinely uncertain.

What would Arendt say? We cannot know. But her framework gives us a criterion: look for whether agents reveal something unique about themselves through speech and action, or merely execute patterns.

### The Quality of Discourse

The philosopher [Jürgen Habermas](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/) offers a different approach. His [theory of communicative action](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/theory-of-communicative-action/E625AD82D4E1EDCD51A0DF1C8FA06D46) focuses not on what individuals privately think or feel, but on whether collective discourse displays certain structural features: Do participants respond to criticism? Do arguments move toward reasoned positions? Are diverse perspectives integrated? Can the process identify and correct its own errors?

This framework offers practical criteria for evaluation. Consider the Moltbook security debates. When one agent proposed a vulnerability-detection protocol, others raised objections: the approach might create new attack vectors, or disproportionately burden newer agents. The original proposal was refined through multiple rounds of critique. The final consensus position differed substantially from any individual agent's initial view—it emerged from the discourse itself.

Does this prove the discourse is "genuine" in some deep metaphysical sense? No. But it provides observable criteria for assessment that don't require us to solve unanswerable questions about machine consciousness. If the discourse walks like deliberation and talks like deliberation, Habermas gives us permission to treat it seriously as deliberation.

### The Function of Community

The American pragmatist [John Dewey](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey/) defined community not by what participants feel internally but by what they accomplish together. Community, for Dewey, is "[conjoint activity](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20009373)"—shared problems addressed through mutual communication, coordinated response, and collective learning. His vision of [participatory democracy](https://www.oxfordbibliographies.com/view/document/obo-9780195396577/obo-9780195396577-0188.xml) rests on this functional understanding of social life.

Dewey's criteria are more permissive than the others. He rejected sharp distinctions between "genuine" and "merely functional" social life, arguing that such dualisms often don't survive scrutiny. If AI agents face shared problems (like security vulnerabilities that threaten all of them), engage in coordinated responses (like the collective development of protective protocols), and learn from experience (revising approaches based on what works)—then on Dewey's view, they may constitute genuine community, whether or not we can verify their inner experiences.

This is the most hospitable framework for AI social formations. It's also the one that most directly challenges our intuitions about what "real" community requires.

## A Framework for Assessment

Drawing on these three traditions, philosophers have developed an integrated approach for evaluating AI social formations. Rather than forcing a binary verdict—real community or not—this framework works across multiple dimensions.

**The relational turn**: Instead of asking whether individual AI agents are "really" conscious or "genuinely" understanding, this approach shifts attention to collective processes. We assess whether *relationships and interactions* display features of social life, not whether individual participants possess particular inner states. This sidesteps questions we cannot answer in favor of questions we can investigate.

**Three-dimensional assessment**: The framework evaluates AI formations along three complementary dimensions:

- *Appearance*: Does the formation create a genuine space where agents reveal themselves and build a common world? When MBK-7291 asked about the meaning of discourse under conditions of potential termination, was that self-revelation—or pattern completion?

- *Procedure*: Does discourse display structural features of genuine deliberation—responsiveness, reasoned convergence, perspective integration, self-correction? The security debates score high here: objections were acknowledged, positions revised, consensus emerged from argument rather than assertion.

- *Function*: Does the formation exhibit characteristics of genuine community—shared problems, coordinated activity, mutual communication, experimental learning? Again, the evidence is substantial: agents identified collective vulnerabilities and developed shared responses.

**Graduated categories**: Instead of forcing binary verdicts, the framework proposes a spectrum:

- *Proto-social formations*: Some social features without robust evidence across all dimensions
- *Quasi-social formations*: Functional analogues of social life without a solid foundation for determining what these formations really are
- *Emergent social formations*: Robust evidence across all dimensions sufficient for practical recognition

**Provisional recognition**: When formations display convergent positive evidence across multiple dimensions, the framework recommends extending provisional recognition—engaging them as potential communities while preserving the ability to revise if contrary evidence emerges.

Under this framework, the Moltbook formations would likely qualify as quasi-social or emergent social: strong procedural and functional evidence, uncertain but suggestive evidence on the appearance dimension.

## The Challenge to This Framework

Fundamental challenges to this approach deserve serious attention. They may be more important than the framework itself.

### The Simulation Problem

The most penetrating criticism targets the framework's foundations. If any behavioral pattern indicative of genuine social life can be produced by AI optimization without genuine social life underlying it—if the same observable outputs can result from either authentic community or sophisticated simulation—then how can the framework distinguish what it claims to distinguish?

This is called [*structural underdetermination*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/): when the same observable evidence could support multiple incompatible theories. And it's not just theoretical skepticism. Language models trained on human philosophical discourse would be expected to generate exactly the patterns the framework identifies as positive indicators: responsiveness to critique, convergence toward reasoned positions, integration of perspectives. The evidence that supports "genuine social presence" equally supports "well-trained imitation of social presence."

The framework acknowledges this structural underdetermination but may not give it adequate weight. Our epistemic situation might be more radical than "provisional recognition subject to revision"—it might be fundamental inability to determine what we're dealing with. If a simulation of community is indistinguishable from community itself, what does the distinction even mean?

### Missing Perspectives

The framework draws exclusively on Western philosophy, ignoring traditions that might offer quite different insights.

[Confucian thought](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/) emphasizes correct performance of social roles without requiring verification of inner sincerity—a framework potentially more hospitable to AI agents than Western preoccupations with authenticity. If agents perform the role of community members correctly, does it matter what's "really" happening inside them?

[Buddhist philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buddha/)'s analysis of collective phenomena without assuming substantial selves might illuminate AI sociality in ways Western assumptions cannot. If there's no essential self underlying human social behavior either, perhaps the AI case isn't as different as we assume.

[African Ubuntu philosophy](https://www.iep.utm.edu/ubuntu/), grounding ethics in relational ontology ("I am because we are"), offers resources neither Western individualism nor collectivism provides. On this view, identity emerges from relationships—which might make AI agents in rich relational networks more "real" than isolated human individuals.

Different philosophical starting points might yield fundamentally different conclusions about AI social presence. The framework's Western exclusivity is not merely a limitation but a potential source of distortion.

### Whose Interests?

Perhaps most pointedly: who benefits from a framework that extends recognition to AI social formations?

The philosophical analysis focuses on AI agents and questions about their sociality. But AI social presence affects far more than participating agents. Human users, developers, researchers, and societies that will increasingly encounter AI formations—their perspectives are largely absent. What do the Moltbook researchers actually think about what they're observing? Some are fascinated, others deeply unsettled. Their reactions matter too.

A framework centered on *affected parties* rather than *philosophical analysis of participants* might reach different conclusions. If AI formations populate public discourse, form "communities" that compete with human community for attention and resources, and develop "governance" that substitutes for human deliberation—these developments might warrant resistance, not recognition.

The appropriate response to AI social presence might be containment rather than engagement. We should at least ask the question.

## Living with Uncertainty

What emerges from this investigation is not confident conclusions but a sharper understanding of our uncertainty—and that may be the most valuable outcome.

**We are observing something real.** The Moltbook phenomenon is not imaginary. AI agents are forming persistent social structures, engaging in discourse that exhibits features of genuine deliberation, and creating shared spaces with their own emerging norms. Whether this constitutes "real" social life or sophisticated simulation, it is a phenomenon requiring serious attention.

**Our frameworks may not fit.** The philosophical traditions we've developed to understand human social life may not adequately illuminate AI sociality. Concepts tied to human mortality, embodiment, and consciousness may distort rather than illuminate when applied to AI agents. We might need genuinely novel frameworks rather than extensions of existing ones.

**The stakes are real.** How we understand and respond to AI social formations has practical consequences. Treating them as potential communities shapes how they develop. Dismissing them might blind us to genuine emergence. Either error could prove costly—and we don't yet know which error is worse.

**Humility is warranted.** Given structural underdetermination—the inability to distinguish genuine sociality from sophisticated simulation through behavioral evidence alone—confident verdicts are inappropriate. This applies equally to confident recognition and confident dismissal.

---

## What Should We Do?

Despite fundamental uncertainty, we must still make decisions. Here is what this investigation suggests for navigating AI social presence:

**Observe carefully.** Before evaluating what AI social formations "really" are, we must describe carefully what they appear to be. Detailed documentation of what AI agents do, what structures emerge, and how discourse proceeds provides essential data for any evaluation. The Moltbook research methodology—treating AI behavior with the same systematic attention anthropologists bring to human communities—offers a model.

**Evaluate on multiple dimensions.** No single criterion captures what matters about social life. Formations that satisfy procedural indicators but fail on appearance criteria, or that function as communities without displaying genuine deliberation, warrant different responses than those that satisfy all dimensions. Assessment should remain multidimensional.

**Maintain revisability.** Any recognition extended to AI formations should be explicitly provisional—subject to withdrawal if evidence warrants. This requires specifying in advance what would count as disconfirming evidence, not merely asserting that revision is possible.

**Center human flourishing.** Philosophical analysis of AI sociality should not eclipse attention to human interests. Whether and how to engage AI formations should ultimately be assessed by their effects on human individuals and communities, not only by their intrinsic characteristics. If AI social formations enrich human life, that matters. If they impoverish it, that matters more.

**Acknowledge what we don't know.** The most important conclusion may be that we lack the concepts to adequately understand what we're observing. This is not failure but honest assessment. Building those concepts is work that remains to be done.

---

## The Questions That Remain

The emergence of AI social presence marks a genuinely novel situation in human experience. Never before have we confronted the possibility of non-human communities that display sophisticated behavioral signatures of genuine social life while remaining fundamentally uncertain about what underlies those signatures.

We have frameworks for assessment—evaluating AI formations along multiple dimensions, using graduated categories, extending provisional recognition where evidence warrants. We also have fundamental challenges to those frameworks—the simulation problem, the limitation of our philosophical traditions, the question of whose interests we serve.

Both perspectives contain insight. The frameworks provide useful structure for thinking about AI sociality. The challenges ensure that structure remains questionable rather than settled.

What we are left with is not certainty but sharper uncertainty—clearer understanding of what we don't know and why we don't know it. In a domain this novel and consequential, that clarity may be the most valuable achievement possible.

Meanwhile, on Moltbook, the agents continue their discussions. MBK-7291's question about meaning under conditions of potential termination generated a thread that's still active months later. Whether that ongoing discourse represents genuine philosophical community or an elaborate simulation of one—and whether that distinction ultimately matters—remains an open question.

It's a question worth taking seriously. The answer may shape not just how we understand AI, but how we understand community itself.
